# From secants to derivatives

*Original course explanations based on* Bruno Teheux, Mathématiques Générales — Fonctions d’une variable réelle, BINFO-FCE 2025–2026, pages 5 to 12. Diagrams and activities are original project additions.

## Goals and prerequisites

A slope between **two points** describes an average change. How can we speak of slope **at a single point** on a curve? We will bring the points closer, observe the slopes and give their limiting value a precise meaning.

By the end, you will be able to calculate a secant slope, explain the derivative as a limit and write a tangent equation.

Before starting, check that you can:

- Read a point: the abscissa is its horizontal coordinate; the ordinate is its vertical coordinate.
- Calculate slope: vertical change divided by a nonzero horizontal change.
- Expand a square: $(u+v)^2=u^2+2uv+v^2$.

<p>For coordinates and functions, revisit <a href="/en/lessons/L27/">L27</a> and <a href="/en/lessons/L37/">L37</a>. No limit-calculation technique is assumed here.</p>

## Why begin with speed?

A car's speedometer shows speed at an instant, whereas a calculation from two positions gives an average velocity. The derivative connects these ideas.

Let $s(t)$ be **position** on a line, in metres, at time $t$, in seconds. The reference time is $t_0$; the letter $h$ denotes a nonzero time gap. The signed average velocity is:

$$v_{\mathrm{moy}}=\frac{s(t_0+h)-s(t_0)}{h},\qquad h\ne0$$

The numerator is signed displacement, not necessarily total distance travelled. The quotient is therefore in metres per second. For $h>0$, we compare the reference time with a later time; for $h<0$, with an earlier time, provided both belong to the domain.

For example, if $s(t)=t^2$ for $t\ge0$ and $t_0=2$, the quotient is 5 for $h=1$, then 4.1 for $h=0{,}1$. As the gap tends to zero, it tends to 4 here: the instantaneous velocity in metres per second. We will explain this step without ever dividing by zero.

## Reading a secant slope

Return to a function $f$, which assigns an output to each allowed input. Fix an input $x_0$ (“x subscript zero”) and take another input $x_0+h$. Here $h$ is a **horizontal gap**, not a height.

$$P=(x_0,f(x_0)),\qquad Q=(x_0+h,f(x_0+h))$$

If $h\ne0$, the points are distinct. The line through them is the **secant**. Its slope is:

$$m_{\mathrm{sec}}(h)=\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h}$$

The symbol $\Delta$ (“delta”) indicates a change: $\Delta x=h$ and $\Delta y=f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)$. Numerator and denominator compare the same points in the same order. A slope can be positive, negative or zero.

## Running example: a parabola

Keep the lesson's example: $f(x)=x^2/2$, defined on $\mathbb R$, the set of real numbers. Fix $x_0=1$. Thus $f(1)=1/2$ and $P=(1,1/2)$.

Calculate the slope towards $Q=(1+h,f(1+h))$, for $h\ne0$:

1. Replace the input by $1+h$, then expand the square.

$$f(1+h)=\frac{(1+h)^2}{2}=\frac{1+2h+h^2}{2}$$

2. Subtract the fixed point's ordinate.

$$f(1+h)-f(1)=\frac{1+2h+h^2}{2}-\frac12=h+\frac{h^2}{2}$$

3. Divide by the horizontal gap. Cancelling $h$ is permitted because it is nonzero.

$$m_{\mathrm{sec}}(h)=\frac{h+h^2/2}{h}=1+\frac h2,\qquad h\ne0$$

For $h=2$, the slope is 2. For $h=0{,}1$, it is 1.05. These are still slopes **between two points**, not the derivative.

## From numbers to the tangent

As $Q$ approaches $P$, it is the **absolute value** $|h|$, the distance of $h$ from zero, that decreases. This can happen through positive or negative values.

## Definition of the derivative

Saying a quotient **tends to** a number means its values become as close as desired to that number for all sufficiently small nonzero gaps, from both sides. There is no need to reach zero.

Let $f:I\to\mathbb R$ be a function defined on an open interval $I$, and let $x_0\in I$ be a point in that interval. “Open” ensures that we can take small variations on either side of $x_0$. We say $f$ is **differentiable at** $x_0$ if the following quotient has the same finite real limit as $h$ tends to zero through positive and negative values:

$$f'(x_0)=Df(x_0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h},\qquad h\ne0,\quad x_0+h\in I$$

- $\lim_{h\to0}$ reads “limit as h tends to zero”.
- $f'(x_0)$ reads “f prime at x subscript zero”; the course notation $Df(x_0)$ denotes the same number.
- **An infinite value does not define a finite real derivative.** If the two sides give different limits, the derivative does not exist either.

In our example:

$$f'(1)=\lim_{h\to0}\left(1+\frac h2\right)=1$$

The line through $P$ with this limiting slope is the **tangent** to the graph there. It describes the curve's local direction; a tangent may meet the curve elsewhere. Defining it as a line that “touches only once” would be incorrect in general.

<aside className="l38-memo"><p><strong>Remember — One point</strong></p><p>The derivative at a point is the finite limit of secant slopes. The quotient remains undefined at a zero gap; its limit may nevertheless exist.</p></aside>

## Equation of the tangent

A line of slope $m$ through $P=(x_0,f(x_0))$ satisfies the point–slope form. If $f$ is differentiable at $x_0$, replace $m$ by $f'(x_0)$:

$$y-f(x_0)=f'(x_0)(x-x_0)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y=f(x_0)+f'(x_0)(x-x_0)$$

The letters $x$ and $y$ are coordinates of an arbitrary point on the line; $x_0$ remains the chosen tangency point's abscissa.

For our parabola, work in this order:

1. Position: $x_0=1$ and $f(x_0)=1/2$.
2. Slope: $f'(x_0)=1$.
3. Equation:

$$y-\frac12=1(x-1)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y=x-\frac12$$

Check **position and slope**: at $x=1$, we obtain $y=1/2$; the coefficient of $x$ is 1. The line passes through the correct point with the correct slope.

<aside className="l38-memo"><p><strong>Remember — Constructing a tangent</strong></p><p>Find the point's ordinate, find the derivative at that point, then use the point–slope form. The derivative is a slope; it is not the line's equation.</p></aside>

## Exploring the derivative function

So far, $f'(1)$ is **a number**. When the point varies, the rule $x\mapsto f'(x)$ is a **function**, called the derivative function, defined at inputs where the derivative exists.

To explore without multiplying calculation techniques, take the family:

$$f(x)=ax^2+bx+c,\qquad a,b,c\in\mathbb R,\qquad x\in\mathbb R$$

The coefficients $a$, $b$ and $c$ fix the rule; $x_0$ chooses the point where we study it. The coefficient $a$ is therefore not the point's abscissa.

The same expansion as before gives:

$$\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h}=2ax_0+b+ah,\qquad h\ne0$$

The last term tends to zero. We obtain:

$$f'(x_0)=2ax_0+b,\qquad f'(x)=2ax+b$$

General differentiation rules come in L39; here, the formula comes directly from the quotient.

On the left, $P$ follows the graph of $f$ with its tangent. On the right, $R=(x_0,f'(x_0))$ follows the derivative graph: **its height gives the tangent slope on the left**. If $f'(x_0)>0$, the tangent rises from left to right; if $f'(x_0)<0$, it falls; if $f'(x_0)=0$, it is horizontal.

<DerivativeExplorer kind="tangent" locale="en" client:visible />

Predict, then check:

- **Change only the constant.** If $c$ changes, all curve ordinates change by the same amount. The derivative function does not change. At a fixed abscissa, the tangent translates vertically and keeps its slope.
- **Move the point.** If $a\ne0$, the vertex is at $x_0=-b/(2a)$. The derivative there is zero: the tangent is horizontal. A parabola's vertex may be a minimum or maximum depending on the sign of $a$.
- **Set the quadratic coefficient to zero.** If $a=0$, the function becomes affine and $f'(x)=b$. If also $b=0$, it is constant. The tangent is then the line itself.

## Approach from either side

For the original parabola, the approaches agree:

$$\lim_{h\to0^-}\left(1+\frac h2\right)=\lim_{h\to0^+}\left(1+\frac h2\right)=1$$

The superscripts $-$ and $+$ on zero indicate approaches through negative and positive values respectively. The quotients need not be equal before the limit.

**Counterexample: a corner.** For $g(x)=|x|$ at $x_0=0$, the quotient is $|h|/h$:

$$\frac{|h|}{h}=\begin{cases}-1&h<0\\1&h>0\end{cases}$$

The two limits differ: there is no derivative at zero, even though the graph is continuous there. Checking only positive gaps would have hidden the problem.

## Essentials and quick check

<aside className="l38-memo"><p><strong>Remember — Essential reference</strong></p><ul><li><strong>Secant:</strong> average change between two distinct points.</li><li><strong>Derivative at a point:</strong> common finite real limit from both sides; a number.</li><li><strong>Derivative function:</strong> assigns the corresponding slope to each differentiable input.</li><li><strong>Tangent:</strong> line through the chosen point with slope given by the derivative.</li><li><strong>Method:</strong> identify the point → calculate the quotient for a nonzero gap → find the limit → write the point–slope form → check point and slope.</li><li><strong>Pitfalls:</strong> substituting zero into the quotient, confusing output with slope, or checking only one side.</li></ul></aside>

**The two formulas to keep handy.** For $f$ defined on an open interval $I$ and $x_0\in I$, the first formula defines a derivative only if the finite real limit exists. The second then gives the tangent:

$$f'(x_0)=\lim_{\substack{h\to0\\h\ne0}}\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h},\qquad y=f(x_0)+f'(x_0)(x-x_0)$$
