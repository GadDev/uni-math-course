# The biconditional and semantic equivalence

## Your objective

You will learn to require **both implications at once**, then distinguish two questions: “do these formulas agree in this case?” and “do they agree in every case?”.

By the end, you will be able to explain and build the biconditional truth table, read “if and only if”, and use a complete table or a counterexample to decide an equivalence.

**Before starting:** <a href="/en/lessons/L04/">L04 — equivalence and tautologies</a>, <a href="/en/lessons/L05/">L05 — implication</a> and <a href="/en/lessons/L06/">L06 — converse</a>.

- An assertion is true (1) or false (0). Letters $A$ and $B$ here denote assertions, possibly compound formulas.
- $\neg A$ means “NOT A”; $A\land B$, “A AND B”; $A\lor B$, “A OR B”, with inclusive OR: at least one is true.
- $A\Rightarrow B$ means “if A, then B”. It is false only at $A=1$, $B=0$. Its converse is $B\Rightarrow A$.
- An **assignment** chooses a value for each variable in a formula. A **tautology** is 1 for every assignment.

**Quick check:** if A and B are false, what are the values of both implications? Both are 1: neither has a true antecedent and a false consequent. If this surprises you, revisit L05 before continuing.

**To work on paper:** use your browser’s Print command. Experiments will be replaced by fixed examples, and hints and solutions will be visible. You can print first, cover the solutions and attempt the exercises.

## Why both directions?

Return to the cards from L05. For a fixed card, A says “it is red” and B says “it has a stamp”. The rule “if it is red, it has a stamp” still allows a blue card with a stamp.

Add the converse rule: “if it has a stamp, it is red”. We now want **red with a stamp, or blue without a stamp**. A red card without a stamp fails in the first direction; a blue card with a stamp fails in the other.

This double commitment motivates a new connective. It does not require both assertions to be true: it requires them to have **the same value**.

## The biconditional

**Definition.** The **biconditional**, written $A\Leftrightarrow B$, is the assertion defined by

$$
(A\Rightarrow B)\land(B\Rightarrow A).
$$

Read it as “A if and only if B”. The connective $\Leftrightarrow$ builds a new assertion. To calculate its value, calculate both implications, then their AND. Since AND requires two values of 1, **both directions must hold**.

## Where does the table come from?

With two independent variables, each has two possible values: there are four assignments. Keep each variable’s value fixed throughout a row. In the table, 0 means false and 1 means true.

| A | B | A ⇒ B | B ⇒ A | A ⇔ B |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| 0 | 1 | 1 | 0 | 0 |
| 1 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 0 |
| 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |

**Build row 10:** A is true and B false. The implication from A to B fails, so it is 0. In the other direction, antecedent B is false, so the implication is 1. Their AND is 0.

**Pause at row 00:** both antecedents are false, so neither implication is contradicted. Their AND is 1. We are saying that **the values agree**, not that A or B is true!

Rows 01 and 10 are disagreements; 00 and 11 are agreements. We have derived the reading rule: **the biconditional is true exactly when both sides have the same value**.

## Reading “if and only if”

“A if B” expresses the direction from B to A. “A only if B” expresses the direction from A to B: B is necessary for A. “A if and only if B” combines both.

In this course, $\mathbb N=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ denotes the natural numbers, including zero. For a fixed natural number, “it is even if and only if it is divisible by two” combines two guarantees: being even ensures divisibility by two, and divisibility by two ensures being even.

However, “being divisible by four” implies “being even”, but the converse fails at 2. To prove an “if and only if” property for every natural number, justify **each direction for every integer in the domain**. A few experiments are insufficient.

## Two different levels

Saying “same value” is not enough: **at which assignment, or at every assignment?** Introduce letters $P$ and $Q$ for two formulas we want to compare. They may contain the same variables; always evaluate them with the same assignment.

| Notation | What we do | What to examine |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $P\Leftrightarrow Q$ | Build an assertion | A value of 0 or 1 on each row |
| $P\equiv Q$ | Assert semantic equivalence | Agreement of both formulas on every assignment |

The symbol $\equiv$ is a **relation between formulas**, not a connective to calculate in a cell. Equivalence means that both result columns agree everywhere. Intermediate calculation columns need not be identical.

**An example not to generalize too quickly:** when A and B are false, an implication and its converse are both 1. But at row 01, the first is 1 and its converse 0: the formulas are not semantically equivalent.

The precise link is this: $P\equiv Q$ holds **exactly when** $P\Leftrightarrow Q$ is a tautology.

### Why is this criterion true?

The justification uses only the definitions, in both directions.

1. **Start with equivalence.** If $P\equiv Q$, their values are identical at every assignment. At each one, $P\Leftrightarrow Q$ is therefore 1. This last formula is a tautology.
2. **Start with a tautology.** If $P\Leftrightarrow Q$ is 1 at every assignment, its two sides have the same value at each one. This is exactly the definition of $P\equiv Q$.

This is a general proof for formulas of classical propositional logic. The following experiment lets you see the mechanism on particular formulas.

## How to proceed, step by step

**When should you choose a table?** For propositional formulas with few variables: it is an exhaustive, mechanical method. With $k$ distinct variables, there are $2^k$ assignments, since each variable offers two choices. The number of rows grows quickly.

1. **Identify both formulas.** Name them P and Q and copy their parentheses.
2. **List the variables appearing in at least one formula.** If only A and B occur, use rows 00, 01, 10 and 11. A shared variable keeps the same value in both formulas.
3. **Calculate from the inside out.** Add columns for difficult subformulas.
4. **Compare both final columns.** A difference provides a counterexample and suffices to refute equivalence. If you have found only agreements, continue until you have covered every assignment.
5. **Write a justified conclusion.** “All rows agree” establishes equivalence; “row … differs” refutes it. The biconditional column makes this comparison visible.

**Useful check:** the last column is 1 wherever P and Q are equal, including when both are 0. Do not replace this test with an AND between P and Q.

## Guided example: testing an equivalence

Test $(A\Rightarrow B)\equiv(\neg A\lor B)$. Here, $P=(A\Rightarrow B)$ and $Q=(\neg A\lor B)$. Compare them on all four assignments of A and B.

| A | B | A ⇒ B | ¬A ∨ B | (A ⇒ B) ⇔ (¬A ∨ B) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| 0 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 1 |
| 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |

Check row 10 especially: $\neg A\lor B=\neg1\lor0=0\lor0=0$. The implication is also 0, so their biconditional is 1, not 0.

Both columns agree on every row. The last column is therefore always 1: it is a tautology, and equivalence is established.

### Recover the formula without a table

An implication fails exactly when A is true and B false. It therefore expresses the negation of that failure. Using the laws from L04:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(A\Rightarrow B)&\equiv\neg(A\land\neg B)\\
&\equiv(\neg A\lor\neg\neg B)\\
&\equiv(\neg A\lor B).
\end{aligned}
$$

The first step comes from the unique failure case; the second applies De Morgan; the third removes double negation. Each transformation preserves values for every assignment. This derivation explains the result verified by the table.

This guided work, whose solution is visible, supports understanding; it does not measure independent mastery.

## Trap: mixing the two levels

- **“Both sides must be true.”** No: the biconditional is also true at row 00. A conjunction is not.
- **“One implication is enough.”** No: at row 01, one implication is true and its converse false. Both directions count.
- **“One row where the formulas agree proves equivalence.”** No: every assignment is needed. However, a single difference refutes equivalence.
- **“Equivalent formulas must be tautologies.”** No: $A\equiv\neg\neg A$, but both sides are false when A is false. Their biconditional is the tautology.

Saying that $A\Leftrightarrow B$ is 0 on a row does not give the relation $A\equiv B$ a value “on that row”: the row witnesses that the two tables do not agree everywhere.

### 1. Calculate one row

For $A=0$ and $B=1$, calculate both implications and then the biconditional. Identify the direction that fails.

<summary>Explained solution — exercise 1</summary>

$A\Rightarrow B$ is 1 because A is false. $B\Rightarrow A$ is 0 because B is true and A false. So $A\Leftrightarrow B$ is $1\land0=0$. The direction from B to A fails.

### 2. Decide with a complete table

Test $\neg(A\land B)\equiv(\neg A\lor\neg B)$. Calculate both sides on all four assignments, then their biconditional. Justify your conclusion.

<details className="l07-exercise">
<summary>Hint — respect the parentheses</summary>

On the left, calculate AND first, then negate its result. On the right, negate each input, then apply inclusive OR.

</details>
<details className="l07-exercise">
<summary>Explained solution — exercise 2</summary>

| A | B | ¬(A ∧ B) | ¬A ∨ ¬B | Biconditional |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| 0 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| 1 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| 1 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 1 |

Agreement on every row establishes equivalence. On the last row, both sides are 0, but their biconditional is 1. This recovers a De Morgan law from L04.

</details>

### 3. Refute a reasoning error

Someone claims: “An implication and its converse are equivalent: I checked row 11.” Find a counterexample and explain why their method is insufficient.

<details className="l07-exercise">
<summary>Hint — separate the two values</summary>

On a row where A and B have the same value, both directions hold. Try an assignment where their values differ.

</details>
<details className="l07-exercise">
<summary>Explained solution — exercise 3</summary>

At row $A=0$, $B=1$, implication $A\Rightarrow B$ is 1 and its converse $B\Rightarrow A$ is 0. The formulas are therefore not equivalent in general. Agreement at 11 did not cover the other assignments.

</details>

### 4. Build another expression

Explain why $A\Leftrightarrow B$ is equivalent to $(A\land B)\lor(\neg A\land\neg B)$. Justify the true and false cases, or use a table.

<details className="l07-exercise">
<summary>Hint — describe both agreements</summary>

How do you write “both are true”? And “both are false”? Inclusive OR combines these two possibilities.

</details>
<details className="l07-exercise">
<summary>Explained solution — exercise 4</summary>

$A\land B$ is 1 only at 11; $\neg A\land\neg B$ is 1 only at 00. Their OR is therefore 1 exactly at 00 and 11, and 0 at 01 and 10. This is the biconditional column: equivalence has been proved for every assignment.

</details>

## Takeaway

**Revision sheet:**

- **Build:** $A\Leftrightarrow B$ is defined by $(A\Rightarrow B)\land(B\Rightarrow A)$.
- **Calculate:** identical values give 1; different values give 0. In order 00, 01, 10, 11, the column is **1, 0, 0, 1**.
- **Distinguish:** $\Leftrightarrow$ builds an assertion; $\equiv$ compares two formulas’ results on every assignment.
- **Decide:** $P\equiv Q$ exactly when $P\Leftrightarrow Q$ is a tautology. One disagreement refutes; agreements establish equivalence only after an exhaustive check or a general proof.
- **Check:** both sides can be false together. Equivalent does not mean “always true”.
