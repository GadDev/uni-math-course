# Coordinates and distances

Coordinates are an address; distance measures the direct route between two addresses. To connect them, build a right triangle: coordinate differences give its legs, then Pythagoras gives the required length.

## Objectives and prerequisites

- Read coordinates.
- Calculate a displacement.
- Calculate a distance.
- Use a midpoint.
- Describe a locus.
- Check coordinate units.

Prerequisites: L23. Domains and restrictions stated below are part of every rule.

**Reminder from [L23](../L23/).** A square root is nonnegative; the square of a negative number is positive. Pythagoras states that in a right triangle the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of squares of the other two sides. A subscript such as $x_A$ means “horizontal coordinate of point A”.

## 1. Understand — An address and a route

Imagine a city map drawn on a grid. One street to the right represents one unit, one street upwards one unit. To find a friend, you need their address; to know the direct route, you need a distance. These are different questions.

A **Cartesian coordinate frame** consists of two perpendicular graduated axes. Their intersection is the origin $O=(0,0)$. A point is located by an **ordered** pair $(x,y)$: the horizontal coordinate x indicates horizontal position, the vertical coordinate y indicates vertical position.

| Sign | Horizontal (x) | Vertical (y) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Positive | Right | Up |
| Negative | Left | Down |
| Zero | On the vertical axis | On the horizontal axis |

To plot $A=(3,2)$, start at O, move 3 units right and 2 up. Point $B=(2,3)$ is not at the same location: order matters. The plane $\mathbb R^2=\mathbb R\times\mathbb R$ contains every pair of real numbers, not only the integer grid points.

**Today's question:** if you go from $A=(1,1)$ to $B=(4,5)$, how long is the direct route? We are not looking for B's address or the length of a detour along the streets.

The hypotenuse is the side opposite the right angle. An **orthonormal** frame has two perpendicular axes with the same length unit on both axes. These are the conditions that allow us to read the two legs directly from coordinate differences.

## 3. Calculate — One operation at a time

**PDF example:** $A=(1,1)$ and $B=(4,5)$.

The Greek letter $\Delta$ (delta) means “change”. Always subtract departure from arrival. Subscripts A and B identify the point concerned, not multiplication.

| Step | Calculation | Why? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Horizontal | $\Delta x=x_B-x_A=4-1=3$ | Go from 1 to 4 |
| 2. Vertical | $\Delta y=y_B-y_A=5-1=4$ | Go from 1 to 5 |
| 3. Squares | $3^2=9,\quad 4^2=16$ | Apply Pythagoras |
| 4. Sum | $d^2=9+16=25$ | Calculate the square of the length |
| 5. Root | $d=\sqrt{25}=5$ | Recover the nonnegative length |

The general method from the PDF is:

$$d(A,B)=\sqrt{(x_B-x_A)^2+(y_B-y_A)^2}=\sqrt{(\Delta x)^2+(\Delta y)^2}.$$

**What if we return from B to A?** The displacements are $(-3,-4)$. The lengths of the legs are 3 and 4, but the signed displacements are negative. Retain the signs until squaring:

$$d(B,A)=\sqrt{(-3)^2+(-4)^2}=\sqrt{9+16}=5.$$

Distance is symmetric and always nonnegative. It is zero if and only if the points coincide. For $(2,-1)$ and $(2,4)$, the displacement is $(0,5)$: the segment is vertical and its length is 5.

**Midpoint — the point halfway along.** For $A=(0,2)$ and $B=(6,4)$, average the horizontal coordinates and then the vertical coordinates, never a horizontal coordinate with a vertical one.

$$M=\left(\frac{0+6}{2},\frac{2+4}{2}\right)=(3,3).$$

In general, $M=((x_A+x_B)/2;(y_A+y_B)/2)$.

**Circle — a locus of points (project extension).** A circle centred at O with radius 5 contains exactly the points at distance 5 from O. Is point $P=(-3,4)$ on it?

$$OP=\sqrt{(-3)^2+4^2}=\sqrt{9+16}=5.$$

Yes. It is in quadrant II, yet its distance from O is positive. For a point with coordinates $(x,y)$, the circle of radius 5 satisfies $x^2+y^2=25$. The disk also includes the interior: $x^2+y^2\le25$. Centre and radius are given, and the radius of a nondegenerate circle is strictly positive.

**Watch the scales — convert before measuring.** With perpendicular axes, if one horizontal unit is 2 m and one vertical unit is 1 m, the coordinate displacement $(3,4)$ represents physical legs of 6 m and 4 m.

$$D=\sqrt{6^2+4^2}=\sqrt{52}\ \mathrm m.$$

Let $s_x>0$ and $s_y>0$ be metres per unit of each axis. The displacements in metres are $X=s_x\Delta x$ and $Y=s_y\Delta y$. The general physical-unit formula is $D=\sqrt{(s_x\Delta x)^2+(s_y\Delta y)^2}$. Changing metres per unit changes the physical situation; merely zooming the image does not change mathematical distances.

### 2. Combine

For $A=(-2;1)$ and $B=(1;5)$, calculate displacement, distance and midpoint in an orthonormal frame.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Retain signs until squaring; average coordinates separately.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Explained solution</summary>

Horizontal: $1-(-2)=3$. Vertical: $5-1=4$. Displacement is $(3;4)$. Distance is $\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=\sqrt{25}=5$. The midpoint is $M=((-2+1)/2;(1+5)/2)=(-1/2;3)$. In reverse, differences change sign but distance does not.

</details>

</div>


<div className="algebra-exercise">

### 3. Reason

The axes are perpendicular. One horizontal unit is $2$ m and one vertical unit is $1$ m. Which physical length corresponds to a change $(3;4)$?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Express both displacements in metres before squaring.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Explained solution</summary>

Physical displacements are $6$ m and $4$ m. The length is $\sqrt{6^2+4^2}=\sqrt{52}$ m, not $5$ m. The formula on raw coordinates assumed the same unit on both axes.

</details>

## 6. Remember — Printable revision sheet

- Coordinates: x horizontal, y vertical; read the signs and preserve the order.
- Displacement from A to B: $\Delta x=x_B-x_A,\quad\Delta y=y_B-y_A$.
- Distance in an orthonormal frame: $d(A,B)=\sqrt{(\Delta x)^2+(\Delta y)^2}$.
- Midpoint: $M=((x_A+x_B)/2;(y_A+y_B)/2)$.
- Circle centred at O, positive radius r: $x^2+y^2=r^2$; disk: $x^2+y^2\le r^2$.
- Different physical scales, perpendicular axes: $D=\sqrt{(s_x\Delta x)^2+(s_y\Delta y)^2}$.
- Traps: swapping coordinates, omitting parentheses around a negative number, confusing detour with direct segment, forgetting the square root or units.

**Self-check:** plot a point in quadrant II, redo the distance calculation with the points swapped, then explain why the right-angled route differs from the direct segment.
