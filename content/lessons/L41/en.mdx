# The natural logarithm

*Original course explanations based on* Bruno Teheux, Mathématiques Générales — Fonctions d’une variable réelle, BINFO-FCE 2025–2026, pages 16, 17. The added explanations, diagrams and activities are original project work.

## Goals and prerequisites

A logarithm answers a question about an exponent. Its derivative answers a question about a slope. By the end, you will connect the two, differentiate a composition, construct a tangent and check a domain.

Before starting, check: $e^0=1$; a slope is a vertical change divided by a horizontal change; differentiating a composition uses the chain rule.

Need a reminder? Revisit <a href="/en/lessons/L38/">L38 — secants and derivatives</a>, <a href="/en/lessons/L39/">L39 — differentiation rules</a> or <a href="/en/lessons/L40/">L40 — the exponential</a>. A guided derivative refresher is also included here.

## The logarithm recovers the exponent

Imagine a machine that takes an exponent $t$ and returns $e^t$. Here $e\approx2{,}71828$ is a constant. The natural logarithm, written $\ln$, retraces the path: it takes a positive number $x$ and recovers the exponent that produced it.

<div class="l41-block">

**Definition — Natural logarithm**

For every real $x>0$, $\ln(x)$ is the unique real number $t$ such that $e^t=x$.

$$\ln(x)=t\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad e^t=x\qquad(x>0)$$

The double arrow means “if and only if”. The exponential and logarithm are **inverse functions**: each undoes the other's action.

$$\ln(e^t)=t\quad(t\in\mathbb R),\qquad e^{\ln(x)}=x\quad(x>0)$$

</div>

The symbol $\mathbb R$ denotes the real numbers. The notation below says that logarithm inputs are strictly positive, while its outputs can be any real numbers:

$$\ln:(0,+\infty)\longrightarrow\mathbb R$$

## Reference values and domain

Start with simple exponential values, then reverse the relationship:

$$e^0=1\ \Longrightarrow\ \ln(1)=0$$

$$e^1=e\ \Longrightarrow\ \ln(e)=1$$

$$e^{-2}=\frac1{e^2}\ \Longrightarrow\ \ln(e^{-2})=-2$$

**Positive input does not mean positive output.** For $0<x<1$, the logarithm is negative; at $x=1$, it is zero; for $x>1$, it is positive.

<div class="l41-block l41-warning">

**Watch out — Check the domain before calculating**

Over the real numbers in this course, neither $\ln(0)$ nor $\ln(-2)$ is defined. In $\ln(u(x))$, the **argument** is the inner expression $u(x)$: solve $u(x)>0$ before calculating.

</div>

Quick question: is $\ln(e^{-2})$ allowed despite the minus sign in the exponent? Explain without a calculator.

<details class="l41-correction">
<summary>Check my answer</summary>

Yes: $e^{-2}>0$. The input is positive and the output equals $-2$.

</details>

## Calculation rules

A logarithm turns a product into a sum: multiplying exponential values adds their exponents. The following rules concern positive inputs; $r$ denotes a real exponent.

$$\ln(xy)=\ln(x)+\ln(y)\qquad(x>0,y>0)$$

$$\ln\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)=\ln(x)-\ln(y)\qquad(x>0,y>0)$$

$$\ln(x^r)=r\ln(x)\qquad(x>0,\ r\in\mathbb R)$$

Example: $\ln(e^2e^3)=\ln(e^5)=5$, recovering $2+3=5$.

**Pitfall:** do not split a sum. Indeed, $\ln(1+1)=\ln(2)>0$, whereas $\ln(1)+\ln(1)=0$.

## Reminder: what does a derivative measure?

A point's height and the slope near that point answer different questions. For $f(x)=\ln(x)$, at input 1 the height is 0; we will see that the slope is 1.

Keep this vocabulary nearby:

- $x$ is the variable, ranging over allowed inputs.
- $a$ is the chosen input where we study the curve; the corresponding point is $A=(a,f(a))$.
- $f(a)$ is the height of that point, a number.
- $h$ is a nonzero horizontal change, positive or negative; the second point is $B=(a+h,f(a+h))$.
- $f'(a)$ is the **derivative at** $a$, a number giving the tangent slope when it exists.
- $f'$ is the **derivative function**: it assigns this number to every input where $f$ is differentiable. The course notation $Df$ also denotes $f'$.

## From secant to tangent

The line through two distinct points $A$ and $B$ is a **secant**. Its slope measures the average change in height per unit of horizontal change:

$$m_{AB}=\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{(a+h)-a}=\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}\qquad(h\ne0)$$

Bringing $B$ towards $A$ reduces the horizontal distance $|h|$. When the slopes approach the same finite number from both sides, that number measures local change: it is the derivative at the chosen input.

<div class="l41-block">

**Definition — Derivative at a point and derivative function**

Let $f$ be defined on an open interval containing $a$. If the following limit exists and is finite, $f$ is **differentiable at** $a$, and we define:

$$f'(a)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$$

The notation $h\to0$ means that $h$ approaches zero through nonzero positive and negative values. **Never substitute zero for $h$ in the quotient.** The function $f'$ collects the derivatives at the inputs where they exist.

</div>

For the logarithm we need $a>0$ and $a+h>0$. In the graph, the dotted corner breaks the displacement into two steps: first $h$ horizontally, then the difference in heights vertically.

<LogarithmExplorer mode="secant" locale="en" client:visible />

At $a=1$, compare $h=0{,}1$ and $h=-0{,}1$, then approach zero. The slopes approach 1 from different sides. A table or graph gives intuition; it does not replace a proof of the limit.

## Why is the derivative of ln equal to 1/x?

The inverse of the exponential is differentiable because the exponential's derivative never vanishes; we accept the inverse differentiation theorem here. We can then recover the formula by differentiating a known identity, using L39's chain rule:

1. Start with $e^{\ln(x)}=x$, for $x>0$.
2. Differentiate both sides: $e^{\ln(x)}\,\ln'(x)=1$.
3. Replace the exponential by $x$: $x\,\ln'(x)=1$.
4. Divide by $x$, which is allowed because $x>0$.

<div class="l41-block">

**Formula — Derivative of the logarithm**

$$f(x)=\ln(x)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f'(x)=D\ln(x)=\frac1x\qquad(x>0)$$

The height $\ln(a)$ can be negative, but the slope $1/a$ is always positive. Do not confuse these two numbers!

</div>

## Method: differentiate a composed logarithm

For a differentiable function $u$ that is strictly positive on the interval under consideration, the chain rule multiplies the outer derivative by the inner derivative:

$$D\ln(u(x))=\frac{u'(x)}{u(x)}\qquad(u(x)>0)$$

<div class="l41-block">

**Method — How do I calculate a derivative?**

Example: $F(x)=\ln(2x+1)$.

1. **Domain:** solve $2x+1>0$, giving $x>-\tfrac12$.
2. **Inside:** identify $u(x)=2x+1$, then $u'(x)=2$.
3. **Chain rule:** write $F'(x)=\frac{1}{2x+1}\times2$.
4. **Result:** $F'(x)=\frac{2}{2x+1}$, still for $x>-\tfrac12$.
5. **Quick check:** at input 0, the slope is $F'(0)=2$. Forgetting the inner derivative would give 1. This check helps find a mistake; it does not prove the formula for every $x$.

</div>

Quick question: why do $F(0)=0$ and $F'(0)=2$ not contradict each other? One is a height, the other a slope.

## Reading the logarithm graph

Return to the activity's graph: the curve passes through $(1,0)$ and $(e,1)$. It rises everywhere because $1/x>0$ for $x>0$. Moving right, the slope decreases: the curve keeps rising but becomes flatter.

$$\lim_{x\to0^+}\ln(x)=-\infty,\qquad \lim_{x\to+\infty}\ln(x)=+\infty$$

The symbol $0^+$ means “towards zero while staying positive”. The line $x=0$ is a **vertical asymptote**: the curve decreases without bound as it approaches, without a value $\ln(0)$ existing. The graph window clips the curve; it does not show its entire domain.

## Method: determine a tangent

A tangent describes the curve's local direction. If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, its nonvertical tangent has slope $f'(a)$ and passes through $(a,f(a))$.

<div class="l41-block">

**Formula — Tangent equation**

$$y=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)$$

$x$ remains variable along the line; $a$, $f(a)$ and $f'(a)$ are fixed. At $x=a$, the term $x-a$ vanishes, recovering the height $f(a)$.

</div>

<div class="l41-block">

**Method — How do I determine a tangent?**

Example: tangent to $f(x)=\ln(x)$ at $a=1$.

1. **Identify the input:** $a=1>0$ belongs to the domain.
2. **Calculate the height:** $f(1)=\ln(1)=0$; the point is $(1,0)$.
3. **Calculate the slope:** $f'(x)=1/x$, so $f'(1)=1$.
4. **Substitute:** $y=0+1(x-1)$.
5. **Simplify and check:** $y=x-1$ passes through $(1,0)$ and its coefficient of $x$ is 1.

</div>

The result is a line, not a new exact expression for the logarithm. Near 1, $\ln(1{,}01)\approx0{,}009950$ and the tangent gives $0{,}01$: the values are close, without being equal.

<LogarithmExplorer mode="tangent" locale="en" client:visible />

To compare slope signs, the activity also offers two transformations, both defined for $x>0$:

$$g(x)=-\ln(x),\quad g'(x)=-\frac1x;\qquad k(x)=(\ln(x))^2,\quad k'(x)=\frac{2\ln(x)}x$$

For $g$, the constant $-1$ stays in front of the derivative. For $k$, the chain rule gives twice the inside, multiplied by its derivative. Try $k$ at $a=1$ for a horizontal tangent, then on either side of 1 to change its slope's sign.

<div class="l41-block l41-warning">

**Watch out — A tangent need not be horizontal**

It is horizontal when the derivative is zero. “Tangent” also does not mean “meets the curve only once”: a tangent can cross a curve again farther away. It describes local direction. Finally, a zero derivative alone does not generally guarantee a maximum or minimum.

</div>

## Solve an equation while preserving the domain

To solve $\ln(x)=2$, first require $x>0$. Then apply the exponential to both sides:

$$\ln(x)=2\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x=e^2$$

The solution is positive, and $\ln(e^2)=2$ verifies the original equation.

Another pitfall: $\ln(x^2)$ is defined for $x\ne0$, because a nonzero square is positive. We cannot replace it by $2\ln(x)$ on its entire domain: the latter excludes negative $x$ inputs. However, the chain rule does give $D\ln(x^2)=2/x$ for $x\ne0$.

## Practise before looking at the answers

Write your answers on paper. For tangents, always separate the height from the slope.

1. **Interpret:** for $f(x)=\ln(x)$ at $a=1$, give the height, slope and tangent direction. Why do the two numbers differ?
2. **Calculate:** find the domain and derivative of $G(x)=\ln(3x-2)$.
3. **Construct:** find the tangent to $f(x)=\ln(x)$ at $a=e$.
4. **Combine:** find the tangent to $F(x)=\ln(2x+1)$ at $a=0$.

<details class="l41-correction">
<summary>A hint before the solution</summary>

For 2, begin with $3x-2>0$. For 3 and 4, first find the point, then the slope; then use point–slope form.

</details>

<details class="l41-correction">
<summary>Worked solutions</summary>

1. $f(1)=0$ is the height; $f'(1)=1$ is the positive slope. The tangent rises. Height locates the point; slope describes local change.
2. $3x-2>0\Longleftrightarrow x>\tfrac23$. The inside has derivative 3: $G'(x)=\frac3{3x-2}$ on this domain.
3. $f(e)=1$ and $f'(e)=1/e$. Thus $y=1+\frac1e(x-e)=x/e$. At $x=e$, we recover 1; the slope is indeed $1/e$.
4. $F(0)=0$ and $F'(0)=2$. Thus $y=0+2(x-0)=2x$. This line passes through the origin and its slope is 2.

</details>

## Essential points to remember

**Revision sheet — ready to print.** The browser's Print menu preserves fixed diagrams and shows the lesson's worked solutions. This sheet starts on a new page.

- **Meaning and domain:** $\ln(x)$ recovers the exponent of $e$; the argument must be strictly positive. $\ln(1)=0$ and $\ln(e)=1$.
- **Derivative:** $f'(a)$ is the finite limit of the quotient $[f(a+h)-f(a)]/h$ as $h\to0$, with $h\ne0$. It is the local slope; $f(a)$ is the height. $f'$ assigns a slope to each input.
- **Rules:** $\ln(xy)=\ln(x)+\ln(y)$ and $\ln(x/y)=\ln(x)-\ln(y)$ for $x,y>0$; $\ln(x^r)=r\ln(x)$ for $x>0$ and $r\in\mathbb R$.
- **Differentiate:** $(\ln x)'=1/x$ for $x>0$. For a differentiable composition: $(\ln u)'=u'/u$ wherever $u>0$. Check the domain, identify the inside, differentiate, apply the chain rule.
- **Tangent at a:** $y=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)$ if $f$ is differentiable at $a$. Calculate the point and slope separately, substitute, then check both.
- **Example:** for $f(x)=\ln x$ at $a=1$, the point is $(1,0)$, the slope is 1 and the tangent is $y=x-1$.
- **Pitfalls:** do not set $h=0$ in the quotient, forget the inner derivative, split $\ln(x+y)$ or confuse a tangent with the whole curve.

**Memory check:** can you explain the difference between height, secant slope and tangent slope aloud, then recover a tangent equation without looking at the method?

</section>
