# Calculating the regression line

## Objectives and prerequisites

- Calculate means and explore a square as an extension.
- Find the best intercept at fixed slope.
- Find the optimal slope.
- Perform a complete fit.
- Predict and check an implementation.
- Handle degenerate cases.

Prerequisites: L22, L26, L34. Domains and restrictions stated below are part of every rule.

<span id="pdf-71" class="source-topic-marker"></span>

## 1. Understand — Choose the line with the smallest error

In L34 our service had three fictional observations: $(0,1),(1,2),(2,2)$. No line passes through all three. Models $\hat y=x+1$ and $\hat y=2$ each have squared loss 1. Can we do better? We seek a slope m and intercept p that minimise the sum of squared vertical residuals.

The theorem in the supplied PDF (p. 68) gives optimal parameters provided the horizontal coordinates are not all equal. First we see why there are two decisions, then calculate the line by hand.

Mean point G is the centre of the data cloud: $G=(\bar x,\bar y)$. For each slope, the best intercept makes the line pass through G. But that alone does not make it the best line among all slopes!

On the three points, line $\hat y=x+2/3$ passes through G but has loss $2/3$. The optimal line $\hat y=(1/2)x+7/6$ has loss $1/6$. In both cases the residuals sum to zero.

## 3. Calculate — The same table, step by step

### A. Three observations: reuse L34 then fit

Step 1: calculate the means.

$$\bar x=(0+1+2)/3=1,\qquad \bar y=(1+2+2)/3=5/3.$$

Step 2: subtract the means, then calculate products and squares.

| $x_i$ | $y_i$ | $x_i-\bar x$ | $y_i-\bar y$ | $(x_i-\bar x)^2$ | $(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 1 | −1 | −2/3 | 1 | 2/3 |
| 1 | 2 | 0 | 1/3 | 0 | 0 |
| 2 | 2 | 1 | 1/3 | 1 | 1/3 |
| Sums | | 0 | 0 | 2 | 1 |

Step 3: add the final two columns separately: $S_{xx}=2$, $S_{xy}=1$. Since $S_{xx}>0$, the slope is defined.

Step 4: apply the theorem. The slope is a quotient; the intercept depends on the slope. Keep exact fractions.

$$m=\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}=\frac12.$$

$$p=\bar y-m\bar x=\frac53-\frac12=\frac76.$$

$$\hat y=\frac12x+\frac76.$$

Step 5: check the mean point by substituting the mean of x: $\hat y(1)=1/2+7/6=5/3$. Step 6: calculate predictions and residuals, as in L34.

| x | y | $\hat y$ | e | $e^2$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 1 | 7/6 | −1/6 | 1/36 |
| 1 | 2 | 5/3 | 1/3 | 1/9 |
| 2 | 2 | 13/6 | −1/6 | 1/36 |

$$S=1/36+1/9+1/36=1/6.$$

The proof below establishes that no other affine line has lower loss. To predict at x = 3: $\hat y(3)=3/2+7/6=8/3$. This extrapolates beyond the range 0 to 2, not an actual measurement.

### C. Result and prediction: reading the fitted line

**Supplied PDF, pages 77–78.** The line fitted using four years is approximately $\hat y=0{,}206x+3{,}31$. The plot distinguishes the four fitting observations from the remaining six. Before coefficient rounding, it passes through mean point $(5{,}3;4{,}4)$.

At $x=2$ pounds of margarine per person per year, displayed coefficients give $\hat y=0{,}206\cdot2+3{,}31\approx3{,}72$ divorces per thousand residents. This is a **numerical model prediction**, outside observed range $[3{,}7;8{,}2]$. It establishes neither causality nor reliable forecasting. We study these limits next.

**Additional example.** At $x=5{,}3$, unrounded coefficients recover exactly $4{,}4$. Checking the mean point is a useful way to catch an intercept error.

## 4. Experiment — Find the minimum

Start with m = 1 and p = 1. Then choose the best p at this slope: the line passes through G and the loss falls. Keep the line anchored at G, change slope, then compare with the exact minimum. The loss plot represents lines through G; its moving marker appears only in that mode.

<RegressionExplorer locale="en" client:visible />

Why does it work? The supplied PDF (pp. 69–72) proves the theorem by completing a square, without differentiation. At fixed slope choose p to pass through G; then minimise a quadratic in m. For our dataset the loss among lines through G is

$$S(m,\bar y-m\bar x)=2(m-1/2)^2+1/6.$$

The square cannot be negative. The minimum is 1/6, attained at slope 1/2. The complete proof and code remain available below.

### Completing the square: building the rewriting

**Proof in the supplied PDF, pp. 69–72, expanded here.** To transform $x^2+6x+1$, seek a square $(x+a)^2=x^2+2ax+a^2$. Coefficient $6$ requires $2a=6$, so $a=3$.

1. Add and subtract the same number: $x^2+6x+1=x^2+6x+9-9+1$.
2. Recognise the square: $(x+3)^2-8$.
3. Use its sign: $(x+3)^2\ge0$, so the value is at least $-8$.
4. Attain it: $x=-3$ makes the square zero, so the minimum is $-8$.

The general recipe is $x^2+bx=(x+b/2)^2-b^2/4$. **Additional example:** $x^2-4x+7=(x-2)^2+3$ has minimum $3$, attained at $x=2$. The drawing shows the lowest point; the calculation proves no other value is smaller. For positive leading coefficient $A$, factor it out first and apply the same idea.

### Additional example: another minimum

Identity $m^2-6m+11=(m-3)^2+2$ shows a minimum of $2$, attained only at $m=3$. More generally, $Am^2-2Bm+C=A(m-B/A)^2+C-B^2/A$ for $A>0$. This method finds the minimum without derivatives because the square is nonnegative.

<span id="supplement-proof-p" class="source-topic-marker"></span>

### Proof, first part: the best p

**Proof in the supplied PDF, pp. 69–72, expanded here.** Fixing $m$ means provisionally choosing a slope and varying only intercept $p$. Put $r_i=y_i-mx_i$, so $e_i=r_i-p$. Expand with the standard identity:

$$S(m,p)=\sum_i(r_i-p)^2=\sum_i r_i^2-2p\sum_i r_i+np^2.$$

Term $p^2$ is constant in the sum, giving $np^2$. Set $\bar r=(1/n)\sum_i r_i$ and rewrite as $n(p-\bar r)^2+\sum_i r_i^2-n\bar r^2$. The last two terms do not depend on $p$. Since $n>0$, the minimum is attained only at $p=\bar r$.

Finally, linearity gives $\bar r=\bar y-m\bar x$. This is precisely the announced intercept. The intercept is fixed for each slope; we must now choose the slope.

<span id="supplement-proof-m" class="source-topic-marker"></span>

### Proof, second part: the best m

**Proof in the supplied PDF, pp. 69–72, expanded here.** Once $p=\bar y-m\bar x$ is chosen, residuals become $(y_i-\bar y)-m(x_i-\bar x)$. Expand each square with $(a-b)^2=a^2-2ab+b^2$, then add: three columns result, $S_{yy}$, $-2mS_{xy}$ and $m^2S_{xx}$.

Loss is therefore a quadratic in $m$ with positive leading coefficient $S_{xx}>0$. Completing the square gives $S_{xx}(m-S_{xy}/S_{xx})^2+S_{yy}-S_{xy}^2/S_{xx}$. Only the first square varies with $m$: making it zero gives $m=S_{xy}/S_{xx}$. Sum of squared deviations $S_{xx}$ is zero only when all horizontal coordinates are equal; handle that case separately.

### Optional supplement: repeating the calculations in Python

The supplied PDF, p. 79, uses all ten observations, rather than just the four in the hand calculation, so the result differs slightly. Function `fit` below implements centred sums with input checks. First define it, then call it on the ten observations.

### Guided example: implementing the calculation

The following code uses centred sums and rejects empty data or constant inputs. For very close floating-point values, a production version must also handle numerical precision. Check passage through the mean point and compare residuals with a small exact example.

```python
def fit(points):
    if not points:
        raise ValueError("empty data")
    n = len(points)
    xb = sum(x for x, y in points) / n
    yb = sum(y for x, y in points) / n
    sxx = sum((x - xb) ** 2 for x, y in points)
    sxy = sum((x - xb) * (y - yb) for x, y in points)
    if sxx == 0:
        raise ValueError("constant inputs")
    m = sxy / sxx
    return m, yb - m * xb
```

```python
x = [8.2, 7.0, 6.5, 5.3, 5.2, 4.0, 4.6, 4.5, 4.2, 3.7]
y = [5.0, 4.7, 4.6, 4.4, 4.3, 4.1, 4.2, 4.2, 4.2, 4.1]
m, p = fit(list(zip(x, y)))
print(round(m, 4), round(p, 4))  # 0.2014 3.3086
```

`zip` pairs values at the same position into data points. `sum` adds values; `** 2` squares a value. Empty data and constant horizontal coordinates are rejected because the formula cannot then identify a unique slope. Another possible implementation is `numpy.polyfit(x, y, 1)`; the detailed version keeps each step visible without another library.

### What if inputs are constant?

If every $x_i$ equals $c$, only value $mc+p$ is observed. Every pair satisfying $mc+p=\bar y$ minimises loss; slope is not identifiable. Do not divide by $S_{xx}=0$. Constant outputs with varying inputs are a different case: the best slope is zero and the intercept is the common output.

## 6. Remember — Printable revision sheet

- Means, centred deviations, products and squares; sum each column.
- $m=S_{xy}/S_{xx}$ if $S_{xx}>0$ ; $p=\bar y-m\bar x$.
- Check the mean point $\hat y(\bar x)=\bar y$ and residuals $\sum e_i=0$.
- Check loss $S=\sum e_i^2$ ; a zero signed sum proves neither quality nor optimality.
- Round at the end; distinguish interpolation, extrapolation and causation.
- If $S_{xx}=0$, the slope is not identifiable.
