# Sigma notation, sums and means

Sigma is shorthand for a long addition: it says where to start, where to stop and which term to add. The mean reuses this sum to share a total equally. We first expand the instructions, then explain why deviations from the mean balance.

## Objectives and prerequisites

- Expand an indexed sum.
- Count terms.
- Use linearity.
- Calculate a mean.
- Centre data.
- Analyse the limitations of a sum.

Prerequisites: L21, L23. Domains and restrictions stated below are part of every rule.

**Reminder from [L21](../L21/) and [L23](../L23/).** Add the values before dividing their total. In a list, $x_i$ denotes the value at position $i$, not a product. The observation count $n$ is a strictly positive integer here: dividing by the size of an empty list would divide by zero.

## 1. Understand — Add, then share

Three journeys take 2, 5 and 8 minutes: the total is 15 minutes and the mean duration 5 minutes. With 100 journeys, writing each addition is tedious. Sigma is a compact instruction for traversing a list. The mean shares the total equally; it need not be an observation in the list.

The PDF, pages 25–26, defines the sum, its properties, the mean and cancellation of deviations. Numerical examples, programming and grouped means are project extensions.

## 3. Calculate — Expand, add, centre

**A — Inclusive bounds and terms.**

For a list, $\sum_{i=1}^n a_i=a_1+\cdots+a_n$. If $a_1=2$, $a_2=5$, $a_3=8$, then $\sum_{i=1}^3a_i=15$. For integer bounds $r\le s$, the term count is $s-r+1$. Example: $\sum_{i=2}^4(2i-1)$.

| i | Term calculation | Running total |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | $2\cdot2-1=3$ | $0+3=3$ |
| 3 | $2\cdot3-1=5$ | $3+5=8$ |
| 4 | $2\cdot4-1=7$ | $8+7=15$ |

$$\sum_{i=2}^4(2i-1)=3+5+7=15.$$

Three terms, not four. Equal bounds give one term. The workshop requires r at most s; outside the workshop, an empty sum is zero by convention, whereas an empty mean is undefined.

Renaming the index everywhere leaves the sum unchanged: $\sum_{i=2}^4(2i-1)=\sum_{j=2}^4(2j-1)$. A constant term is repeated, not replaced by the index: $\sum_{i=2}^4 5=5+5+5=15$.

**B — Linearity.**

**The PDF’s three properties, pages 25–26.** For lists of length $n\ge1$ and a real constant $c$:

1. Termwise addition: $\sum_{i=1}^n(a_i+b_i)=\sum_{i=1}^n a_i+\sum_{i=1}^n b_i$.
2. Constant factor: $\sum_{i=1}^n ca_i=c\sum_{i=1}^n a_i$.
3. Repeated constant: $\sum_{i=1}^n c=nc$.

**Additional examples.** For lists $(2,5)$ and $(1,3)$, the first rule gives $(2+1)+(5+3)=(2+5)+(1+3)=11$. The second gives $3\cdot2+3\cdot5=3(2+5)=21$. The third gives $4+4+4=3\cdot4=12$. These rules follow from addition and distributivity; they require no mysterious new recipe.

Check the first sum using linearity:

$$2\sum_{i=2}^4i-\sum_{i=2}^41=2(2+3+4)-3=15.$$

Only a factor independent of the index can be taken outside this way.

**C — Mean and deviations.**

For $n\ge1$, $\bar x=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n x_i$. The mean retains the data units. For 2, 5 and 8, the mean is 5 and deviations −3, 0, 3. For 1, 2 and 6:

$$\bar x=(1+2+6)/3=3.$$

| $x_i$ | $x_i-\bar x$ | $\lvert x_i-\bar x\rvert$ | $(x_i-\bar x)^2$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | −2 | 2 | 4 |
| 2 | −1 | 1 | 1 |
| 6 | 3 | 3 | 9 |
| Sums | 0 | 6 | 14 |

Signed deviations cancel, but absolute values and squares do not. For 1 and −1, $(1+(-1))^2=0$ but $1^2+(-1)^2=2$.

<details>
<summary>PDF proof (page 26) — why deviations cancel</summary>

**Why does this work for every nonempty list?** The list is fixed during this proof: its mean is therefore a constant, even though the observations differ.

1. Definition: $\bar x=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n x_i$; therefore $\sum_{i=1}^n x_i=n\bar x$.
2. Repeating the same constant $n$ times gives $\sum_{i=1}^n\bar x=n\bar x$.
3. By linearity, $\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)=\sum_{i=1}^n x_i-\sum_{i=1}^n\bar x$.
4. Replacing both sums gives $n\bar x-n\bar x=0$.

Later, $\sum_i$ abbreviates summing over all observations, from $1$ to $n$. This cancellation concerns **signed** deviations, not their absolute values or squares.

</details>

**D — Weighted mean: count individuals.**

| Group | Count | Mean | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 2 | 10 | 20 |
| B | 8 | 20 | 160 |
| Overall | 10 | ? | 180 |

$$\bar x=\frac{2\cdot10+8\cdot20}{2+8}=18.$$

The simple average of the means gives 15. Equal sizes guarantee that this shortcut works; so do identical means. For nonempty groups of sizes $n_j$ and means $m_j$, use $\frac{\sum_j n_jm_j}{\sum_j n_j}$. The total count must be strictly positive.

**E — Prepare regression.**

In L34–L36 we will revisit three fictional observations: $(0,1),(1,2),(2,2)$.

$$\bar x=(0+1+2)/3=1,\qquad \bar y=(1+2+2)/3=5/3.$$

| $x_i$ | $y_i$ | $x_i-\bar x$ | $y_i-\bar y$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 1 | −1 | −2/3 |
| 1 | 2 | 0 | 1/3 |
| 2 | 2 | 1 | 1/3 |
| Deviation sums | | 0 | 0 |

Keeping exact fractions avoids rounding errors. This table prepares centred sums for regression and correlation. L27 also reuses the mean for midpoint coordinates.

## 4. Experiment — A loop and a balance point

The Python equivalent of the sum from 2 to 4 includes 4: range stops before its upper bound, so use 5. The workshop's new table displays the term and total after every iteration.

```python
total = 0
for i in range(2, 5):
    total += 2 * i - 1
print(total)  # 15
```

Predict the result before changing bounds. Compare term 2i − 1 with constant 5; try equal or reversed bounds too.

## 5. Practise — Calculate and justify

### 1. Apply

Expand $\sum_{i=2}^4 i^2$.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Write the three index values.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Explained solution</summary>

$\sum_{i=2}^4 i^2=2^2+3^2+4^2=4+9+16=29$. The lower bound is included.

</details>

</div>


<div className="algebra-exercise">

### 2. Combine

Calculate the mean of $1,2,6$, then the sum of deviations.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

The mean shares a total of $9$ among three values.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Explained solution</summary>

$\bar x=9/3=3$. Deviations are $-2,-1,3$; their sum is $0$. No individual deviation is zero, yet they cancel.

</details>

</div>


<div className="algebra-exercise">

### 3. Reason

Two groups of sizes $3$ and $1$ have means $10$ and $18$. What is their overall mean?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Recover totals before sharing.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Explained solution</summary>

$(3\cdot10+1\cdot18)/(3+1)=48/4=12$. The unweighted mean $14$ would give equal weight to groups of different sizes.

</details>

## 6. Remember — Printable revision sheet

<div className="algebra-revision">

- Read both included bounds, count, substitute, add.
- $s-r+1$ terms for integer bounds satisfying $r\le s$.
- $\sum_{i=1}^n(a_i+b_i)=\sum_{i=1}^na_i+\sum_{i=1}^nb_i$ ; $\sum_{i=1}^nca_i=c\sum_{i=1}^na_i$ ; $\sum_{i=1}^nc=nc$.
- $\bar x=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nx_i$ for $n\ge1$ ; $\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)=0$.
- Groups: recover totals as count × mean, then divide by overall count.
- Do not confuse sum of squares and square of sum; keep units and exact fractions.
