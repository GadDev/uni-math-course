# Cartesian equations and curves

An equation acts as a gatekeeper: each point presents its two coordinates, and calculation decides whether it belongs to the curve. The drawing helps us see; equality lets us decide. Before continuing, can you substitute a negative number and read a pair $(x,y)$ in coordinates? Revisit [L24](../L24/) and [L27](../L27/) if needed.

## Objectives and prerequisites

- Test membership.
- Recognise a line.
- Recognise a circle.
- Recognise a parabola.
- Compare two equations.
- Detect a nonequivalent transformation.

Prerequisites: L24, L27. Domains and restrictions stated below are part of every rule.

## 1. Understand — A rule that selects points

Imagine a program that receives a pair of coordinates and returns true or false. Its rule is $y=2x+1$. Point $A=(1,3)$ proposes x = 1 and y = 3. Point $B=(2,4)$ proposes x = 2 and y = 4. The program does not choose randomly: it accepts a point exactly when both sides of the equality have the same value.

**Link to logic and sets (PDF, pp. 31–32).** An equation $E(x,y)$ is a predicate on the real plane. It defines a set by comprehension:

$$\mathcal C=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb R^2\mid E(x,y)\}.$$

Read: “C is the set of real pairs such that E is true.” The bar means “such that”, and $\in$ means “belongs to”. For a given point:

$$(a,b)\in\mathcal C\Leftrightarrow E(a,b).$$

The curve is therefore **the set of all accepted points**, not just a few points in the drawing.

- $y=2x+1$: a line. For one extra unit of x, y increases by 2.
- $x^2+y^2=4$: a circle centred at O with radius 2.
- $y=x^2$: an upward-opening parabola; $(-1,1)$ and $(1,1)$ illustrate symmetry.
- $x=y^2$: a right-opening parabola; $(1,-1)$ and $(1,1)$ have the same horizontal coordinate.

A curve is not always the graph of a function $y=f(x)$: a horizontal parabola may have two vertical coordinates for one horizontal coordinate, and $x=2$ describes a vertical line. An equation can also define multiple branches, a single point or no real point. A square alone is not enough to identify a parabola.

## 3. Calculate — Substitute, compare, conclude

### Test the points from the PDF

For $A=(1,3)$ and $B=(2,4)$, test $y=2x+1$. Do not recalculate y and silently replace the given coordinate: compare the proposed value with the required value.

| Step | A | B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Left side: given y | $3$ | $4$ |
| 2. Right side: substitute x | $2\cdot1+1$ | $2\cdot2+1$ |
| 3. Calculate | $3$ | $5$ |
| 4. Compare | $3=3$ | $4\ne5$ |
| 5. Conclude | $A\in\mathcal C$ | $B\notin\mathcal C$ |

**General method:** substitute coordinates for every variable, calculate each side separately, compare and conclude. Visual proximity is not proof of membership.

### Why a circle? (PDF, p. 34)

In L27, we saw that in an orthonormal coordinate system the distance from O to point P is the square root of the sum of squared coordinates. A radius-2 circle selects exactly the points at distance 2:

$$x^2+y^2=4\Leftrightarrow\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=2\Leftrightarrow d(P,O)=2.$$

The nonnegative square root allows the transformation in both directions. Point $(0,2)$ fits because $0^2+2^2=4$; point $(0,1)$ does not, even though it is inside the disk.

### Read a shifted circle (extension)

For centre $C=(a,b)$ and point $P=(x,y)$, Pythagoras gives:

$$CP^2=(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2.$$

Requiring $CP=r$ with $r>0$ gives $(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2=r^2$.

In $(x-2)^2+(y+1)^2=9$, the centre is $(2,-1)$: the sign in the parentheses is opposite to that of the centre coordinate. The radius is $\sqrt{9}=3$, not 9. To test $(5,-1)$:

$$(5-2)^2+(-1+1)^2=9+0=9.$$

If r = 0, the set reduces to its centre. A negative right side gives no real point. The disk includes the interior and boundary: $(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2\le r^2$.

### One curve, several equations (PDF, p. 35)

Two equations are equivalent if they have exactly the same solutions. Start with the equation from the PDF:

$$y=2x+1\Leftrightarrow2x-y+1=0\Leftrightarrow4x-2y=-2.$$

First transformation: subtract y from both sides, then reverse the equality. Second: subtract 1, then multiply by 2. We can reverse these operations because 2 is nonzero. Similarly, $2x+2y=6$ and $x+y=3$ are equivalent.

**Careful:** an implication is not necessarily an equivalence. For equation $y=x$, squaring gives $y^2=x^2$. But point $(1,-1)$ satisfies $(-1)^2=1^2$ without satisfying $-1=1$. The new equation contains both lines $y=x$ and $y=-x$. The added point is a counterexample.

Multiplying $y=x$ by x gives $xy=x^2$, which is also true for every point on line $x=0$. For example $(0,2)$ is added. Dividing by a variable expression can instead lose solutions: handle the case where it vanishes separately. For a square root, also respect the domain and sign: $y=\sqrt{x}$ requires $x\ge0$ and $y\ge0$.

**Drawing** means going from an equation to points; **modelling** means starting with points or a situation to find an equation. The next lessons do both with lines. Testing a few points does not establish general equivalence; justify both directions.

## 5. Practise — From a simple test to a counterexample

**Easy.** Does point $(2,5)$ lie on line $y=2x+1$?

<details>
<summary>Check the answer</summary>

Yes: the left side is 5 and the right side is $2\cdot2+1=5$. The two sides are equal.

</details>

**Intermediate.** Give the centre and radius of $(x+1)^2+(y-2)^2=4$. Is point $(1,2)$ on the circle?

<details>
<summary>Check the answer</summary>

The centre is $(-1,2)$ and the radius is 2. The test gives $(1+1)^2+(2-2)^2=4$, so yes. The radius is not 4.

</details>

**Challenge.** For $y=x$, does dividing both sides by x give an equivalent equation over the whole real plane? Give the restriction and one lost point.

<details>
<summary>Show the worked solution</summary>

Division requires $x\ne0$ and gives $y/x=1$. Point $(0,0)$ satisfies the original equation, but the quotient is undefined there. Retain the case $x=0$ separately; here it forces $y=0$.

</details>

## 6. Remember — Printable revision sheet

- Membership: $(a,b)\in\mathcal C\Leftrightarrow E(a,b)$. Substitute, calculate both sides, compare.
- Line: $ax+by=c$ with $(a,b)\ne(0,0)$; if $b=0$, it is vertical.
- Circle: $(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2=r^2$ in orthonormal coordinates, centre $(a,b)$, radius $r>0$; disk: inclusive inequality.
- Parabolas: $y=x^2$ (upward), $x=y^2$ (rightward).
- Equivalence: justify both directions. One added or lost point is enough to disprove it.
- Traps: dividing by zero, forgetting restrictions, confusing radius with squared radius, confusing circle and disk.
