# Rasterisation and Bresenham’s idea

The ideal segment is continuous; the image consists of cells. We therefore choose an approximation, including a tie rule. Revisit continuous coordinates versus pixels ([L32](../L32/)), then the sign of an affine expression ([L31](../L31/)). The activities explicitly state vertical orientation.

## Objectives and prerequisites

- Sample by column.
- Choose the traversal axis.
- Handle endpoints and direction.
- Interpret the midpoint test.
- Update an integer decision.
- Respect the algorithm’s limits.

Prerequisites: L31, L32. Domains and restrictions stated below are part of every rule.

<span id="pdf-56" class="source-topic-marker"></span>

## 1. Understand — Turn a line into pixels

Imagine drawing a diagonal in a pixel-art game. A mathematical line is continuous, but the screen can only light whole cells. **Rasterising** means selecting pixels to represent the segment as closely as possible.

In the supplied PDF (pp. 56–57), the naive algorithm calculates one height per column and rounds it. Our running example is the segment from $(0,0)$ to $(5,2)$, with slope $m=2/5$. Two questions: which pixels should we select, and how can we avoid multiplication, division and rounding at each step?

The following uses an upward vertical axis, as in the PDF's Bresenham explanation. Do not confuse it with the downward v axis in L32.

### Choose E or NE: the next-pixel game

In the Bresenham explanation (supplied PDF, p. 59), the vertical axis points up. Endpoints are $(0,0)$ and $(u_2,v_2)$, integers with $u_2>0$ and $0\le v_2\le u_2$. The PDF also denotes these displacements by $\Delta x=u_2$ and $\Delta y=v_2$. We work in one octant.

From the current pixel $(u,v)$, advance one column. Two candidates: $E=(u+1,v)$, to the right, and $NE=(u+1,v+1)$, diagonally upwards. Their midpoint is $M=(u+1,v+1/2)$.

If the line passes below M, choose E; if it passes above M, choose NE. In a tie, both candidates are equally close. The application variant chooses E. This explicit tie rule belongs to the application; the supplied PDF does not specify equality on this page.

## 3. Calculate — From rounding to an integer decision

### A. Sample and choose the axis

For our segment the ideal height is $v=(2/5)u$.

| Column u | Ideal height | Rounded height |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 1 | 0.4 | 0 |
| 2 | 0.8 | 1 |
| 3 | 1.2 | 1 |
| 4 | 1.6 | 2 |
| 5 | 2 | 2 |

We obtain six pixels, including endpoints. For slope $8/3$, the heights rounded by column are $0,3,5,8$: there are gaps. Traversing rows, calculate $x=(3/8)y$ and the horizontal coordinates become $0,0,1,1,2,2,2,3,3$.

In screen notation, $v=v_1+m(u-u_1)$ becomes $u=u_1+(v-v_1)/m$ if $m\ne0$. Choose columns if $|\Delta x|\ge|\Delta y|$ and $\Delta x\ne0$; otherwise choose rows if $\Delta y\ne0$. A vertical keeps its column; an isolated point gives one pixel. From $(4,1)$ to $(4,5)$, keep column 4 and rows 1 to 5. For negative displacement, traverse the axis in the corresponding direction.

In the sampling workshop, halfway ties round to the greater integer, unlike the Bresenham workshop's E convention.

<span id="pdf-61" class="source-topic-marker"></span>

### B. Understand the sign of d (project extension)

The supplied PDF introduces the sign of affine expression $\Delta y x-\Delta x y$ (p. 59), as in L31. We develop this into an integer test.

The line's height at the next column is $(v_2/u_2)(u+1)$; M's height is $v+1/2$. Choose E when the midpoint height is greater than or equal to the ideal height.

$$v+1/2\ge(v_2/u_2)(u+1).$$

Multiplying both sides by $2u_2>0$ preserves the inequality direction. We obtain $u_2(2v+1)\ge2v_2(u+1)$. Rearranging:

$$d=2v_2(u+1)-u_2(2v+1).$$

This value gives the sign of line-minus-midpoint height difference, multiplied by $2u_2$. It is not a distance in pixels. If $d\le0$, choose E; if $d>0$, choose NE. In exercises, D and d name the same decision.

<span id="pdf-62" class="source-topic-marker"></span>

### C. Initialise, decide, update

Initially $u=v=0$, so $d=2v_2-u_2$. For our example, $u_2=5$, $v_2=2$:

- Initial decision: $d=2\cdot2-5=-1$.
- After E: add $2v_2=4$.
- After NE: add $2v_2-2u_2=-6$.

The first pixel is already $(0,0)$. The decision is the value **before** moving.

| Current | d before | Choice | Increment | Next | d after |
| --- | ---: | --- | ---: | --- | ---: |
| (0,0) | −1 | E | +4 | (1,0) | 3 |
| (1,0) | 3 | NE | −6 | (2,1) | −3 |
| (2,1) | −3 | E | +4 | (3,1) | 1 |
| (3,1) | 1 | NE | −6 | (4,2) | −5 |
| (4,2) | −5 | E | +4 | (5,2) | −1 |

Example of the second step: d is 3, so NE. Increase both coordinates by 1, then $d=3-6=-3$. At the final pixel, stop: the resulting d requires no further decision.

**Tie.** For $u_2=2$, $v_2=1$, the initial decision is 0: E leads to $(1,0)$, then NE to $(2,1)$. Rounding to the greater integer would choose $(1,1)$ at the first step. Both conventions are possible, but do not mix them.

<span id="pdf-63" class="source-topic-marker"></span>

### D. Go further — Why do the increments work?

After E, only u increases by 1:

$$d_E=2v_2(u+2)-u_2(2v+1)=d+2v_2.$$

After NE, u and v increase by 1:

$$d_{NE}=2v_2(u+2)-u_2(2v+3)=d+2v_2-2u_2.$$

The common terms cancel. This is the PDF's idea: replace repeated calculations with integer additions.

### For developers — From table to Python code

This original project implementation uses the E tie convention. It returns pixels instead of lighting them. Both doubled values are calculated before the loop.

```python
def raster(u2, v2):
    if not isinstance(u2, int) or not isinstance(v2, int):
        raise ValueError("integer endpoints required")
    if not (u2 > 0 and 0 <= v2 <= u2):
        raise ValueError("requires u2 > 0 and 0 <= v2 <= u2")
    twice_u = 2 * u2
    twice_v = 2 * v2
    d = twice_v - u2
    v = 0
    pixels = []
    for u in range(u2 + 1):
        pixels.append((u, v))
        if d > 0:
            v = v + 1
            d = d - twice_u
        d = d + twice_v
    return pixels
```

Each iteration adds one pixel. If the decision is strictly positive, it moves up one unit and subtracts the horizontal double; in every case, it adds the vertical double. This is not an all-octant implementation. For an isolated point, display it directly; for other directions, transform coordinates or use a general implementation.

## 5. Practise — From pixel to trace

**Easy.** For $(0,0)$ to $(2,1)$, calculate the initial decision, the first choice and the next decision.

<details>
<summary>Check the answer</summary>

$d=2\cdot1-2=0$. Choose E, giving $(1,0)$, then $d=0+2=2$. Equality does not require NE in this variant.

</details>

**Intermediate.** Trace $(0,0)$ to $(4,2)$ by hand, including both endpoints.

<details>
<summary>Check the answer</summary>

Initially $d=2\cdot2-4=0$. E gives $(1,0)$ and d is 4; NE gives $(2,1)$ and d is 0; E gives $(3,1)$ and d is 4; NE gives $(4,2)$. Heights are $0,0,1,1,2$.

</details>

**Challenge.** For $(0,0)$ to $(3,8)$, explain why the integer variant presented does not apply directly. Which sampling axis should be chosen and how many pixels result?

<details>
<summary>Show the worked solution</summary>

$8>3$: the slope is too steep for this variant. Sampling must traverse rows, from 0 to 8 inclusive, giving nine pixels. An adapted Bresenham implementation may swap axes; simply using the same formulas without transformation is insufficient.

</details>

## 6. Remember — Printable revision sheet

- Domain: integers, start $(0,0)$, $u_2>0$, $0\le v_2\le u_2$, vertical upwards.
- Initialise: $d=2v_2-u_2$. Choose E if $d\le0$, NE if $d>0$.
- E: increment u by 1, keep v, add $2v_2$ to d.
- NE: increment u and v by 1, add $2v_2-2u_2$ to d.
- Include both endpoints; compare before moving, update after, stop at the final pixel.
- Sampling: choose the axis with greater absolute displacement; handle vertical and isolated point separately.
- Traps: confuse d with a distance, change tie rule, forget axis orientation or apply this variant outside its octant.
