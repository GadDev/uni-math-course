# A limit a code cannot cross

Could an even cleverer code beat Huffman and go below entropy? For a binary uniquely decodable code and a fixed source, no. Here is why, without skipping the PDF's normalisation factor.

**Source: PDF, pp. 49–54**, using the tools from pp. 45–48. The result concerns average length, not each isolated symbol.

## Objectives and prerequisites

You will: (1) state assumptions; (2) normalise binary weights; (3) apply Gibbs; (4) follow the inequality chain; (5) characterise equality; (6) explain a strict gap. Prerequisites: [L51](/en/lessons/L51/) and [L52](/en/lessons/L52/).

## 01 · Objects used in the proof

A source has a finite alphabet of at least two symbols with $p_i>0$ and $\sum_i p_i=1$. A binary uniquely decodable code assigns nonempty words of lengths $\ell_i$. Recall

$$L(c)=\sum_i p_i\ell_i,\qquad H(S)=-\sum_i p_i\log_2p_i.$$

No temporal independence is needed for this symbol-by-symbol bound. Unique decodability is essential, however: the impossible code giving the same short word to every symbol could have a small mean but would not transmit the information.

## 02 · Build a distribution from lengths

By McMillan, $K=\sum_i2^{-\ell_i}\le1$. Every term is positive, so $0<K\le1$. The weights do not necessarily sum to 1: they are not yet probabilities. Define

$$q_i=\frac{2^{-\ell_i}}K.$$

Every term is positive and

$$\sum_iq_i=\frac1K\sum_i2^{-\ell_i}=1.$$

This is exactly what Gibbs requires. Directly replacing q by the unnormalised weights would be wrong if their sum is strictly below 1.

## 03 · The full chain

Gibbs applied to p and q gives

$$H(S)\le-\sum_i p_i\log_2q_i.$$

Expand the logarithm: $\log_2q_i=-\ell_i-\log_2K$. Thus

$$-\sum_i p_i\log_2q_i=\sum_i p_i\ell_i+\log_2K\sum_i p_i=L(c)+\log_2K.$$

Probabilities sum to 1. Finally, logarithms increase and $K\le1$, so $\log_2K\le0$. We obtain

$$H(S)\le L(c)+\log_2K\le L(c).$$

**Watch the sign:** there is a **plus** before the logarithm of K; since this logarithm is negative or zero, the middle term does not exceed L. This proves the announced bound.

## 04 · When is the bound attained?

Both inequalities must become equalities. Gibbs requires $p_i=q_i$ for every i; the second requires $K=1$. Together:

$$L(c)=H(S)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p_i=2^{-\ell_i}\quad(\forall i).$$

Probability normalisation then automatically gives Kraft sum 1. Probabilities must therefore be inverse powers of two matching integer lengths. Saying only “the code is Huffman” does not guarantee equality.

- For $(1/2,1/4,1/4)$ and words `0`, `10`, `11`, we have $K=1$ and $L=H=3/2$.
- For $(1/2,1/2)$ and words `00`, `01`, we have $K=1/2$, $q=p$ and $H=1=L+\log_2K< L=2$. Gibbs is an equality, but the code leaves space unused.
- For $(2/3,1/3)$ and words `0`, `1`, Huffman gives $L=1$ while $H\approx0{,}9183$. The code is symbol-by-symbol optimal; the gap remains strict because probabilities are not dyadic.

An individual length may be smaller than its symbol's surprise: the bound concerns the **weighted average**. It also does not say that every observed message costs at least its symbol count times entropy.

## Exercises and solutions

1. Reconstruct the proof without looking: what are the two normalisations?
2. For probabilities $(1/2,1/4,1/4)$ and lengths $(2,3,3)$, calculate K, q and all three terms.
3. Can a uniform three-symbol source have average length equal to entropy with binary symbol-by-symbol coding?

<details className="info-answer">
<summary>Justified solutions</summary>

1. p sums to 1 by assumption; binary weights become q after division by K. Gibbs followed by McMillan completes the proof.
2. $K=1/4+1/8+1/8=1/2$, $q=(1/2,1/4,1/4)$ and $H=3/2$, $L+\log_2K=3/2$, $L=5/2$. Only the second inequality is strict.
3. No: $1/3$ is not an inverse power of two with integer exponent. Huffman gives lengths 1, 2, 2 with mean $5/3>\log_2 3$.

</details>

## Remember and continue

Assumptions → McMillan → normalisation → Gibbs → nonpositive logarithm. Equality needs both a complete tree and probabilities matching its depths. [L54](/en/lessons/L54/) shows how block coding reduces the per-symbol gap.
