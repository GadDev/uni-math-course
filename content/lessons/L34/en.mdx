# Affine models and residuals

An affine rule can predict without passing exactly through observations. Choosing a model requires measuring what it misses and comparing all observations with the same criterion. Revisit sums ([L26](../L26/)) and reading $m$ and $p$ ([L29](../L29/)).

## Objectives and prerequisites

- Calculate a prediction.
- Calculate a signed residual.
- Explain error cancellation.
- Calculate squared loss.
- Calculate centred sums.
- Compare model criteria.

Prerequisites: L26, L29. Domains and restrictions stated below are part of every rule.

## 1. Understand — Predicting is not observing

Imagine a program predicting service waiting time from load. Three fictional observations will guide the entire lesson: $(0,1),(1,2),(2,2)$. Model $\hat y=x+1$ predicts 3 at load 2, but we measure 2. How do we measure the gap?

In the supplied PDF, pp. 62–64, ten observations associate margarine consumption and divorce rates. No line passes through every point; a criterion is needed to compare candidates. We seek $y\approx mx+p$. Association does not prove causation. The [original table](../L36/#pdf-65) returns in L36.

## 3. Calculate — One operation at a time

### A. Predict, subtract, square

Definition in the supplied PDF, pp. 65–66:

$$\hat y_i=mx_i+p,\qquad e_i=y_i-\hat y_i=y_i-(mx_i+p).$$

For model $\hat y=x+1$, the last observation is $(2,2)$. Prediction is $1\cdot2+1=3$. Residual is $2-3=-1$. The model overestimates; the square is $(-1)^2=1$.

| i | x | Observed | Predicted | Residual | Square |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0 | 1 | $0+1=1$ | $1-1=0$ | 0 |
| 2 | 1 | 2 | $1+1=2$ | $2-2=0$ | 0 |
| 3 | 2 | 2 | $2+1=3$ | $2-3=-1$ | 1 |

The sum of squares is 0 + 0 + 1 = 1. Sigma means repeat and add the terms. Here n is 3.

$$S=e_1^2+e_2^2+e_3^2=\sum_{i=1}^3e_i^2=1.$$

Careful: squaring each residual before adding is not squaring the sum.

### B. Why squares? Three criteria

The supplied PDF (p. 66) compares signed sum, absolute sum and squared sum. Apply them to the same three observations.

| Model | Residuals | Signed | Absolute | Squared |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\hat y=x+1$ | 0, 0, −1 | −1 | 1 | 1 |
| $\hat y=2$ | −1, 0, 0 | −1 | 1 | 1 |
| $\hat y=(1/2)x+7/6$ | −1/6, 1/3, −1/6 | 0 | 2/3 | 1/6 |

For the third model, predictions are $7/6,5/3,13/6$. Signed errors cancel, but $1/36+1/9+1/36=1/6$. This is not perfect prediction. More simply, residuals −2 and +2 give signed sum 0, absolute sum 4 and squared sum 8. The signed sum can become arbitrarily negative as p increases. Absolute error is possible; squares are convenient algebraically and penalise larger deviations more.

### C. Least squares: one global loss

The supplied PDF, p. 67, defines regression by minimising

$$S(m,p)=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-mx_i-p)^2,\qquad n\ge1.$$

Project extension: mean squared error is $\mathrm{MSE}=S/n$. For the first two models it is $1/3$ ; for the third, $1/18$. On the same data, minimising S or MSE is equivalent. If $S=0$, every prediction is exact. A zero signed sum is insufficient. Doubling a residual quadruples its square. The third model beats the other two according to S, but this does not prove optimality.

### D. Centring: move the origin to the data cloud

<figure class="teaching-figure">
<img src="/diagrams/droites/en/l34-centering.svg" alt="Three points are translated so G(1,5/3) becomes the origin." width="640" height="300" loading="lazy" />
<figcaption>Centring preserves the cloud's shape; only the origin changes.</figcaption>
</figure>

A bar denotes a mean. Calculate $\bar x=(0+1+2)/3=1$ and $\bar y=(1+2+2)/3=5/3$. Then subtract these means from each point.

| $x_i$ | $y_i$ | $x_i-\bar x$ | $y_i-\bar y$ | $(x_i-\bar x)^2$ | $(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)$ | $(y_i-\bar y)^2$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 1 | −1 | −2/3 | 1 | 2/3 | 4/9 |
| 1 | 2 | 0 | 1/3 | 0 | 0 | 1/9 |
| 2 | 2 | 1 | 1/3 | 1 | 1/3 | 1/9 |
| Sums | | 0 | 0 | 2 | 1 | 2/3 |

The last three columns give the notation from the supplied PDF, p. 67:

$$S_{xx}=\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)^2$$

$$S_{xy}=\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)$$

$$S_{yy}=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\bar y)^2$$

Squared sums are never negative; the sum of products can be negative. If all x are equal, $S_{xx}=0$. The slope formula in L35 requires separate treatment of this case.

## 6. Remember — Printable revision sheet

- Prediction: $\hat y_i=mx_i+p$ ; residual: $e_i=y_i-\hat y_i$.
- Positive: underestimate; negative: overestimate.
- Loss: $S=\sum e_i^2\ge0$ ; MSE = $S/n$ for $n>0$.
- Centring: calculate means, subtract, form squares and products.
- $S_{xx}=\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2$, $S_{xy}=\sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)$, $S_{yy}=\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2$.
- Traps: reverse residual, square the entire sum, confuse cancellation with perfect fit, confuse fit with causation.
