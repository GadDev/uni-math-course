# Affine models and residuals

An affine rule can predict without passing exactly through observations. Choosing a model requires measuring what it misses and comparing all observations with the same criterion. Revisit sums ([L26](../L26/)) and reading $m$ and $p$ ([L29](../L29/)).

## Objectives and prerequisites

- Calculate a prediction.
- Calculate a signed residual.
- Explain error cancellation.
- Calculate squared loss.
- Calculate centred sums.
- Compare model criteria.

## The model: why compare several lines?

**PDF situation, pages 65–67.** The ten margarine/divorce observations form a scatterplot; no line passes through all of them. The [starting data table](../L36/#pdf-65) opens this part in the PDF path. Seek an approximate relationship $y\approx mx+p$, rather than an equality for every observation.

A line can pass near one point and far from others. To compare models fairly, apply the **same criterion** to every observation. The green line is fitted to all ten points; the dashed line is another candidate. The drawing poses the question, then residuals let us measure the answer.

**Additional example.** For $(0,1),(1,2),(2,2)$, compare $\hat y=x+1$ and $\hat y=2$. The first misses the last point by one unit; the second misses the first by one. Here they have equal sums of squared errors despite making different predictions.

## A model need not equal the data

At each input $x_i$, the model predicts $\hat y_i=mx_i+p$. Observation $y_i$ may differ. The residual is $e_i=y_i-\hat y_i$. A positive residual means the observed point is above the line, so the model underestimates that observation. We fit vertical errors, not perpendicular distances from the line.

## Measuring error: observation, prediction and residual

The hat in $\hat y_i$, read “y hat subscript i”, marks the **prediction** for observation number $i$. Value $y_i$ without the hat is **observed**. The PDF defines residual $e_i=y_i-\hat y_i=y_i-(mx_i+p)$.

**Additional detailed example.** With $x_i=2$, $y_i=6$, $m=2$ and $p=1$, the model predicts $\hat y_i=2\cdot2+1=5$. Residual $e_i=6-5=1$: the observed point is one unit above the line. With observation $y_i=4$, the same model would give $e_i=-1$. The drawing’s segments are vertical because input $x_i$ stays fixed.

**The residual is defined on page 68; the criterion comparison below is supplemental.** Adding signed residuals can conceal errors: $-2+2=0$. Adding absolute values is another possible criterion: $|-2|+|2|=4$. Least squares chooses $(-2)^2+2^2=8$; errors no longer cancel and large errors weigh more. This is a choice of criterion, rather than the only way to measure error.

## Guided example: calculating residuals

For data $(0,1),(1,2),(2,2)$ and model $\hat y=x+1$, predictions are $1,2,3$. Residuals are $0,0,-1$ and their squared sum is $1$. The final negative residual indicates overestimation. Reversing the sign convention would not change the squares, but would change sign interpretation.

## How to build an error measure

Subscript $i$ numbers observations; $n$ is their count. Symbol $\sum_{i=1}^n$ asks us to add terms for indices 1 through $n$. Start with concrete calculations before compressing them into a formula:

1. At each observed horizontal coordinate, calculate prediction $\hat y_i=mx_i+p$.
2. Subtract **prediction from observation**: $e_i=y_i-\hat y_i$.
3. Square each residual before adding: $S=\sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2$.
4. For an average per observation, divide by $n>0$: $\mathrm{MSE}=S/n$.

Why squares? They are nonnegative, so opposite errors no longer cancel. Doubling a residual makes its contribution four times larger. This choice favours small errors and strongly penalises large ones; absolute error is another possible criterion.

**Check:** $S=0$ implies every square is zero, so every observation equals its prediction. In contrast, $\sum e_i=0$ only says signed errors cancel. The drawing uses vertical segments because the two heights are compared at the same horizontal coordinate.

## A loss that cannot cancel

Least-squares loss is $S(m,p)=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-mx_i-p)^2$, with $n\ge1$. It is nonnegative and equals zero exactly when every prediction is exact on the data. Mean squared error is $\mathrm{MSE}=S/n$. For the same dataset, minimising either gives the same parameters. Squaring amplifies large residuals.

## Guided example: a misleading sum

Residuals $-2,2$ sum to zero but have loss $8$. Residuals $1,1$ sum to $2$ but have loss $2$. Choosing only the smallest signed sum would wrongly favour the first model; very large negative residuals could even make that criterion arbitrarily negative.

## Centring: subtract a mean before multiplying

A bar denotes an **arithmetic mean**: $\bar x=(x_1+\cdots+x_n)/n$ and $\bar y=(y_1+\cdots+y_n)/n$. Centring means subtracting this mean from each value. Positions are then measured relative to the cloud’s centre, rather than zero.

For the activity’s three data points, $\bar x=1$ and $\bar y=5/3$. Here is the exact calculation before rounding:

| $x_i$ | $y_i$ | $x_i-\bar x$ | $y_i-\bar y$ | $(x_i-\bar x)^2$ | $(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $0$ | $1$ | $-1$ | $-2/3$ | $1$ | $2/3$ |
| $1$ | $2$ | $0$ | $1/3$ | $0$ | $0$ |
| $2$ | $2$ | $1$ | $1/3$ | $1$ | $1/3$ |

The last two columns give $S_{xx}=2$ and $S_{xy}=1$. Squaring vertical deviations also gives $S_{yy}=4/9+1/9+1/9=2/3$. Centred deviations sum to zero; their squares can still have a positive sum. These calculations prepare the next lesson’s formula without assuming it is already known.



## Least squares and the three centred sums

**PDF definition, pages 69–70.** Choose parameters $m,p$ to make $S(m,p)=\sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2$ as small as possible. The name means “smallest sum of squares”, rather than choosing the smallest points.

The page-70 notation abbreviates repeated calculations; $S_{yy}$ is supplemental support for the proof and correlation:

| Notation | Calculation | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $S_{xx}$ | $\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)^2$ | Sum of squared horizontal deviations. |
| $S_{xy}$ | $\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)$ | Sum of products of deviations. |
| $S_{yy}$ | $\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\bar y)^2$ | Sum of squared vertical deviations. |

**Additional example.** For $(0,1)$ and $(2,3)$, means are $\bar x=1$, $\bar y=2$. Horizontal deviations are $-1,1$, as are vertical deviations. Hence $S_{xx}=1+1=2$, $S_{xy}=1+1=2$ and $S_{yy}=1+1=2$. The next lesson keeps these columns visible in a four-observation table.

## Three useful sums

The squared sums $S_{xx}$ and $S_{yy}$ are never negative, but the cross sum $S_{xy}$ can be negative. The table above will be reused for the regression line.

For $(0,1),(1,2),(2,2)$, calculate residuals, $S$ and MSE for horizontal model $\hat y=2$. Compare with $\hat y=x+1$ without claiming that the best possible model has been found.

Predictions are $2,2,2$; residuals are $-1,0,0$. Thus $S=1$ and MSE is $1/3$, as for model $\hat y=x+1$. This only establishes a tie between these two candidates. Setting $m=1/2$, $p=7/6$ gives residuals $-1/6,1/3,-1/6$, hence $S=1/6<1$. A good numerical fit proves no causation.

## Printable revision sheet

- **Prediction and residual:** $\hat y_i=mx_i+p$, $e_i=y_i-\hat y_i$; positive means observation above the model.
- **Loss:** $S=\sum e_i^2\ge0$, MSE is $S/n$ for $n>0$; compare on the same observations.
- **Centring:** calculate means, subtract, then form squares and products.
- **Traps:** zero signed sum is not zero loss; vertical residual is not perpendicular distance; fit is not causation.
