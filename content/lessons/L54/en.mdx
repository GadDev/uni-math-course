# Group symbols to approach the limit

Two symbols may each need one bit even when one is much rarer. Can coding pairs use less than one bit **per original symbol**? Yes, without falling below entropy: integer lengths are shared more efficiently across several symbols.

**Source: PDF, pp. 55–60.** The quantitative upper-bound proof below is a teaching addition to the source's statement of Shannon's theorem.

## Objectives and prerequisites

You will: (1) form the block alphabet; (2) calculate probabilities under independence; (3) Huffman-code pairs; (4) distinguish cost units; (5) prove entropy additivity and Shannon's bound; (6) explain the codebook trade-off. Prerequisites: [L49](/en/lessons/L49/) and [L53](/en/lessons/L53/).

## 01 · The alphabet changes, and assumptions matter

Let S have m symbols, with $m\ge2$, strictly positive probabilities and **independent, identically distributed** draws (the iid model). Extension $S^n$ treats each word of n symbols as one new symbol. Its alphabet has $m^n$ blocks.

Independence gives

$$P(s_{i_1}\cdots s_{i_n})=\prod_{j=1}^n p_{i_j}.$$

Without independence, this multiplication is unjustified. For example, a sequence always alternating a and b with an equally likely first symbol has uniform marginals, but only ab and ba can occur in a two-symbol block. Probabilities of aa and bb are not one quarter.

Block length n is known to the decoder. A finite message whose size is not a multiple of n needs an agreed remainder rule; the formulas below describe complete blocks and exclude headers, a transmitted dictionary or final padding.

## 02 · The PDF example with explicit units

Take $p(a)=2/3$, $p(b)=1/3$. Coding individual symbols as `0` and `1` gives mean 1 bit per symbol. For independent pairs:

| Block | Probability | Codeword | Length |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| aa | 4/9 | 0 | 1 |
| ab | 2/9 | 10 | 2 |
| ba | 2/9 | 110 | 3 |
| bb | 1/9 | 111 | 3 |

Huffman merges weights $1+2=3$, then $2+3=5$, then $4+5=9$. Ties allow ab and ba to exchange roles without changing optimal cost.

Write $L_n$ for average length **per block**. Then

$$L_2=\frac49\cdot1+\frac29\cdot2+\frac29\cdot3+\frac19\cdot3=\frac{17}9\text{ bits/bloc}.$$

A block represents two symbols:

$$\frac{L_2}{2}=\frac{17}{18}\approx0{,}9444\text{ bit/symbole}.$$

This improves on 1, but original entropy remains $H(S)\approx0{,}9183$. Page 58 reports cost already converted per character; we separate the two units to avoid comparing 17/9 directly with 1.

## 03 · Why block entropy is multiplied by n

A product logarithm becomes a sum. Write blocks as w with probability P:

$$H(S^n)=-\sum_w P(w)\log_2P(w)=-\sum_wP(w)\sum_{j=1}^n\log_2p_{i_j}.$$

Exchange the two finite sums. For a fixed position j, summing probabilities over other positions gives 1: what remains is the mean surprise of one symbol. Each of the n positions therefore contributes $H(S)$:

$$H(S^n)=nH(S).$$

This identity uses the iid model; it does not follow merely from a block's letter count. For the previous pairs, block entropy is about 1.8366 bits, or 0.9183 bits per symbol.

## 04 · Shannon: bound the per-symbol cost

For every n, construct a Huffman code on blocks. Its optimal mean is $L_n$. The previous bound applied to the block alphabet gives $L_n\ge H(S^n)$.

For the upper bound, first choose Shannon lengths

$$\ell(w)=\lceil-\log_2P(w)\rceil.$$

Every block probability is strictly between 0 and 1 in our setting, so these are positive integer lengths. Rounding upwards gives $2^{-\ell(w)}\le P(w)$. Their sum is at most 1: Kraft guarantees a prefix code with these lengths. Also, $\ell(w)<-\log_2P(w)+1$. Weighting and summing gives a mean strictly below $H(S^n)+1$. Huffman cannot cost more than this code, so

$$H(S^n)\le L_n<H(S^n)+1.$$

Divide by positive n and use additivity:

$$H(S)\le\frac{L_n}{n}<H(S)+\frac1n.$$

The guaranteed per-symbol gap tends to zero. For a positive margin $\varepsilon$, choosing $n>1/\varepsilon$ guarantees a gap strictly below that margin. This is a general guarantee, not the smallest block size needed for a specific source. Costs need not decrease strictly at every n.

## 05 · Codebook cost and the PDF conclusion

The alphabet grows from m symbols to $m^n$ blocks. Storing, building and sharing the codebook costs more; a short message may not offset that overhead. The theorem concerns ideal average codeword cost, not automatically the final size of a real file.

The final page briefly mentions dictionary compression. **Historical correction:** it associates “LZW (1977)” with ZIP. LZ77 dates from 1977; LZW is the variant Welch published in 1984. The DEFLATE format commonly used in ZIP combines LZ77 and Huffman. This locates the families; learning LZW is not an additional chapter objective.

References for this clarification: [Welch, 1984](https://courses.cs.duke.edu/spring03/cps296.5/papers/welch_1984_technique_for.pdf) ; [RFC 1951, DEFLATE](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1951.html).

## Exercises and solutions

1. An iid binary source has probabilities $(3/4,1/4)$. Calculate pair probabilities and Huffman cost per symbol.
2. A size-3 block code averages 7.2 bits per block. What is its cost per symbol?
3. Why not automatically choose block size one million?

<details className="info-answer">
<summary>Justified solutions</summary>

1. Probabilities are $9/16,3/16,3/16,1/16$. Weight merges are $1+3=4$, $3+4=7$, $7+9=16$. Merge weights sum to 27: $L_2=27/16$ bits per block and $L_2/2=27/32$ bits per symbol. Original entropy is about 0.8113: the bound is respected.
2. $7{,}2/3=2{,}4$ bits per symbol. Units require division.
3. The codebook has exponentially many blocks; building, storing and transmitting it becomes impractical. A small theoretical gap does not remove these costs.

</details>

## Remember

Always state assumptions and units: independence for multiplication, identical laws for the additivity used, bits per block before dividing by n. Shannon guarantees approaching entropy from above at the cost of a growing codebook. The full path now connects codes from [L43](/en/lessons/L43/) to their mathematical limit.
