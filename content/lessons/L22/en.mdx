# Expanding and factorising

One quantity can wear different “costumes”: a sum helps with some calculations and a product with others. The goal is to change form to answer a question while preserving value. First follow L21 → L22 → L23 → L24; “Next in the PDF” links offer an alternative reading in source-document order.

## Objectives and prerequisites

- Distribute a factor.
- Factor out a common factor.
- Expand a product.
- Use standard identities.
- Choose a useful form.
- Refute a false identity.

Prerequisites: L21. Domains and restrictions stated below are part of every rule.

**Reminder from [L21](../L21/).** $-2(x+1)$ means the number $-2$ multiplies the whole group. Add coefficients of like terms: $3x+2x=5x$, but $x+x^2$ cannot be collected that way. A letter represents a number; $x^2$ means $x\cdot x$.

## 1. Understand — Two forms, one value

Three bags each contain x items and two extra items. Count by bag, $3(x+2)$, or by item type, $3x+6$. The total is unchanged. Expanding turns a product into a sum; factorising gathers a sum into a product.

A term is a piece separated by addition or subtraction at the outermost level; a factor is a piece multiplied by another. In $3x+6$, the terms are $3x$ and $6$ ; in $3(x+2)$, the factors are $3$ and $x+2$.

## 3. Calculate — Recognise, transform, check

### A. Expand and collect (PDF, pages 7–12)

Professor's example:

$$3(x-2)-2(x+1)$$

| Action | Expression |
| --- | --- |
| Distribute 3 to both terms | $3x-6-2(x+1)$ |
| Distribute −2 to both terms | $3x-6-2x-2$ |
| Collect x terms and constants | $(3-2)x+(-6-2)$ |
| Collect | $x-8$ |

The sign of factor −2 belongs to both products. In the other PDF example, $5-(2x-3)=5-2x+3=8-2x$. The minus before parentheses means multiplication by −1.

For two groups, the general formula is $(a+b)(c+d)=ac+ad+bc+bd$. In our additional example, $(x+2)(x+3)=x^2+3x+2x+6=x^2+5x+6$. Like terms have the same variable part: add their coefficients, but do not mix x and x squared.

Another check: $5(x-2)-3(x+4)=5x-10-3x-12=2x-22$. At x = 2, both forms equal −18; this local check is not a universal proof.

### B. Factorise: find what repeats (PDF, pages 9–10)

In the professor's example, $6x^2-9x$, write each term as a product:

$$6x^2=(3x)(2x),\qquad -9x=(3x)(-3).$$

$$6x^2-9x=3x(2x-3).$$

We factored out a term, not divided by x. The identity still holds at x = 0. To verify, expand back: $3x(2x-3)=6x^2-9x$.

### Standard algebraic identities: understanding all three formulas

An **identity** is an equality true for every value in its domain. For all real $a,b$:

| Recognise this form | Expansion | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $(a+b)^2$ | $a^2+2ab+b^2$ | Two cross products $ab$. |
| $(a-b)^2$ | $a^2-2ab+b^2$ | The final square remains nonnegative. |
| $(a+b)(a-b)$ | $a^2-b^2$ | The cross products cancel. |

Expand the first: $(a+b)^2=(a+b)(a+b)=a^2+ab+ba+b^2$. Since $ab=ba$, the two pieces give $2ab$. The drawing uses positive lengths; the algebraic identity holds for all real numbers. For the second, replace $b$ with $-b$. For the third, expansion gives $a^2-ab+ab-b^2$.

**PDF examples, pages 15–18.** $101^2=(100+1)^2=10000+200+1=10201$. For $(2x-3)^2$, identify $a=2x$ and $b=3$: $(2x)^2-2(2x)(3)+3^2=4x^2-12x+9$.

**Two additional examples.** $49^2=(50-1)^2=2500-100+1=2401$. And $103\cdot97=(100+3)(100-3)=10000-9=9991$. Name the two pieces before choosing a formula.

**Quick check.** Does $(x+4)^2$ contain $8x$? Yes: $2\cdot x\cdot4=8x$. Writing only $x^2+16$ erases the drawing’s two rectangles.

### Recognise the right identity: three clues

To factorise a perfect-square trinomial, do not stop at the first and last squares: check the middle term.

| Expression | Clues | Factors |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $x^2+6x+9$ | $a=x,\ b=3,\ 2ab=6x$ | $(x+3)^2$ |
| $x^2-6x+9$ | $a=x,\ b=3,\ -2ab=-6x$ | $(x-3)^2$ |
| $x^2-9$ | $a=x,\ b=3$ | $(x-3)(x+3)$ |
| $x^2+4$ | No cross term; sum of squares. | None of the three identities in this form. |

### D. Choose a factorisation method

<figure class="teaching-figure">
<img src="/diagrams/droites/en/l22-factor-guide.svg" alt="Look for a common factor and extract it if present; seek a difference of squares or perfect square, repeat if possible and expand to check." width="600" height="340" loading="lazy" />
<figcaption>If no pattern fits, these methods are insufficient: do not invent a factorisation.</figcaption>
</figure>

Two-stage example: $3x^3-12x=3x(x^2-4)=3x(x-2)(x+2)$. Step 1: common factor 3x; step 2: difference of squares; step 3: verify by expanding. The factorised form reveals zeros without dividing by x.

Expanded form helps collect terms, factorised form reveals zeros, square form reveals a sign: $x^2+10x+25=(x+5)^2\ge0$. One counterexample refutes an identity: at a = 2 and b = 3, $(a+b)^2=25$ but $a^2+b^2=13$.

## 5. Practise — Recognise without hints

<div className="algebra-exercise">

### 1. Apply

Expand $-3(2x-5)$.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Distribute the negative factor to both terms.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Explained solution</summary>

$-3(2x-5)=-6x+15$. The two negative signs multiply to give the positive term.

</details>

</div>


<div className="algebra-exercise">

### 2. Combine

Factorise $2x^2-8$.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

A common factor, then a difference of squares.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Explained solution</summary>

$2x^2-8=2(x^2-4)=2(x-2)(x+2)$. Both steps hold for every real number.

</details>

</div>


<div className="algebra-exercise">

### 3. Reason

Choose a form to justify that $x^2-6x+9\ge0$ for every real number.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Recognise both squares and their cross term.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Explained solution</summary>

$x^2-6x+9=(x-3)^2\ge0$. A square is nonnegative. It is zero exactly when $x=3$.

</details>
