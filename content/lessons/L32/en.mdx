# From coordinates to pixels

The same point can have two addresses: its mathematical coordinates and its screen position. We will build the address change, then check the return trip. Revisit coordinates ([L27](../L27/)) and isolating an unknown ([L25](../L25/)).

## Objectives and prerequisites

- Distinguish axis conventions.
- Transform a point.
- Invert the transformation.
- Transform differences and slopes.
- Choose scale and origin.
- Analyse rounding and visibility.

Prerequisites: L25, L27, L29. Domains and restrictions stated below are part of every rule.

## The screen is a grid of pixels

A **pixel** is one cell of an image. In the PDF’s convention, columns $u$ increase rightwards and rows $v$ downwards; the first column and row are numbered zero.

**PDF example, page 52.** Pixel $(5,2)$ lies in the sixth column and third row. The order remains horizontal then vertical, but vertical coordinates increase downwards. In an $8\times6$ image, columns range from $0$ to $7$ and rows from $0$ to $5$: pixel $(8,6)$ is outside the image.

## Two coordinate systems

In the mathematical plane, $y$ increases upward. On the screen considered here, the origin is at the top left, $u$ increases rightward and $v$ downward. Continuous coordinates may be real; pixel indices are integers. An image of width $W$ and height $H$ has indices $0\le u<W$ and $0\le v<H$.

## From mathematical coordinates to the screen: centring

**PDF, pages 53–54.** In an $800\times600$ pixel window, choose the centre as origin: $(u_0,v_0)=(400,300)$. At one pixel per unit, $u=400+x$ and $v=300-y$. The minus is necessary: a higher point must have a smaller row number.

**Additional example.** $(x,y)=(20,10)$ becomes $(u,v)=(420,290)$. Mathematical origin $(0,0)$ becomes $(400,300)$, rather than the upper-left corner.

## Translation, scale and sign

With $s_x>0$ and $s_y>0$ pixels per unit, use $u=u_0+s_xx$ and $v=v_0-s_yy$. The mathematical origin is displayed at $(u_0,v_0)$. The minus sign reverses the vertical direction. To preserve shapes and angles, choose $s_x=s_y$; otherwise a circle appears elliptical.

## Changing units: 50 pixels per unit

**PDF example, page 55.** Keep the centre and choose $50$ pixels per unit: $u=400+50x$ and $v=300-50y$. For $(2,1)$: $u=400+100=500$ and $v=300-50=250$.

**Additional example.** For $(-3,-2)$, obtain $(250,400)$. To invert, undo the centre shift and divide by the scale: $x=(u-400)/50$ and $y=(300-v)/50$. Returning from $(500,250)$ recovers $(2,1)$. This inversion concerns continuous coordinates before rounding to pixels.

## How to build and invert the transformation

A **scale** here is a number of pixels per mathematical unit: $s_x$ horizontally, $s_y$ vertically. Screen point $(u_0,v_0)$ specifies where the mathematical origin is placed.

1. Multiply horizontal displacement by its scale: $s_xx$, then add the origin position: $u=u_0+s_xx$.
2. A positive mathematical height must move up on screen, decreasing row number: $v=v_0-s_yy$.
3. To return, undo the operations in reverse order:

$$u-u_0=s_xx\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=\frac{u-u_0}{s_x},$$

$$v_0-v=s_yy\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y=\frac{v_0-v}{s_y}.$$

Division requires nonzero scales; this convention imposes $s_x>0$ and $s_y>0$. Subscripts 0 identify the chosen screen origin, not multiplication by zero.

**Where does screen slope come from?** Two points give $\Delta u=s_x\Delta x$ and $\Delta v=-s_y\Delta y$. For a nonvertical line, division yields $\Delta v/\Delta u=-(s_y/s_x)m$. The minus sign comes from the orientation change, not a calculation error.

## Guided example: forward and inverse

Take $u=300+40x$ and $v=200-40y$. Point $(-2,3)$ becomes $(220,80)$. To invert, use $x=(u-300)/40$ and $y=(200-v)/40$. Pixel $(380,240)$ therefore corresponds to $(2,-1)$ before any rounding uncertainty.

## Guided example: transforming a line

With the same scales, line $y=2x+1$ becomes $v=760-2u$. Indeed, substitute $x=(u-300)/40$ into $v=200-40(2x+1)$. The screen slope is negative: a stroke rising rightward has decreasing screen vertical coordinates. In general, slope becomes $-(s_y/s_x)m$.

The steps are $v=200-40(2x+1)=160-80x$, then $v=160-80(u-300)/40=160-2u+600=760-2u$.

## Round after calculating

To display a point, calculate continuous coordinates, then apply an explicit rounding rule. Rounding to nearest limits error to half a pixel per coordinate; halfway ties need a convention. Several real points may map to one pixel: rounding is not invertible. An out-of-image coordinate remains out of bounds even when its algebraic transformation is correct.

## Your turn on paper

With $(u_0,v_0)=(200,150)$ and $s_x=s_y=40$, transform $(-2,3)$, then recover the starting point. Why does this round trip stop being exact after rounding?

<details>
<summary>Show the worked solution</summary>

We obtain $u=200+40(-2)=120$ and $v=150-40\cdot3=30$. Returning gives $x=(120-200)/40=-2$ and $y=(150-30)/40=3$. After rounding, several continuous positions can give the same pixel index: lost information cannot be recovered exactly. Also check image bounds after applying the chosen rounding convention.

## Printable revision sheet

- **Transformation:** $u=u_0+s_xx$, $v=v_0-s_yy$, for $s_x,s_y>0$.
- **Continuous inverse:** $x=(u-u_0)/s_x$, $y=(v_0-v)/s_y$.
- **Method:** set origin and scales, calculate, check the inverse, then round using an explicit convention and check visibility.
- **Traps:** omitting the minus; confusing continuous coordinates and integer indices; assuming rounding is invertible.
