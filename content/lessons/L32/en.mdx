# From coordinates to pixels

The same point can have two addresses: its mathematical coordinates and its screen position. We will build the address change, then check the return trip. Revisit coordinates ([L27](../L27/)) and isolating an unknown ([L25](../L25/)).

## Objectives and prerequisites

- Distinguish axis conventions.
- Transform a point.
- Invert the transformation.
- Transform differences and slopes.
- Choose scale and origin.
- Analyse rounding and visibility.

Prerequisites: L25, L27, L29. Domains and restrictions stated below are part of every rule.

## 1. Understand — Two addresses for one point

Imagine programming a game or a chart in HTML Canvas. Your mathematical point has coordinates $(2,1)$, but the screen only knows positions measured in pixels. How do you draw the point in the right place?

**PDF, pp. 52–53.** The screen is a grid of width W and height H. A pixel is a cell; its address $(u,v)$ means column u, row v. Indices start at zero.

<figure class="teaching-figure">
<img src="/diagrams/droites/en/pixels.svg" alt="Grid with eight columns and six rows, origin at top left, cell 5,2 marked with a cross." width="440" height="360" loading="lazy" />
<figcaption>Indices start at zero; v increases downwards.</figcaption>
</figure>

Pixel $(5,2)$ is therefore in the sixth column and third row. In an image of $8\times6$ pixels, valid indices are $0\le u<8$ and $0\le v<6$, with integer u and v. Pixel $(8,6)$ does not exist.

Our objective: transform a mathematical address into a screen address, then recover the initial point as long as no rounding has taken place.

Decompose the transformation into three operations: **change units**, **reverse the vertical direction**, **add the origin position**. The minus sign does not mean the point is necessarily negative: it changes the displacement direction.

## 3. Calculate — One operation at a time

### Forward: the PDF point

Take $(x,y)=(2,1)$, screen origin $(u_0,v_0)=(400,300)$ and scales $s_x=s_y=50$ pixels per unit.

| Step | Horizontal | Vertical |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mathematical coordinates | $x=2$ | $y=1$ |
| Multiply by scale | $50\cdot2=100$ | $50\cdot1=50$ |
| Reverse vertical direction | $+100$ | $-50$ |
| Add origin | $400+100=500$ | $300-50=250$ |

The result is $(u,v)=(500,250)$.

With a chosen origin and positive scales, the general formulas are:

$$u=u_0+s_xx,\qquad v=v_0-s_yy.$$

Subscripts 0 identify the position of the mathematical origin on screen. Scales are pixels per unit. Each screen coordinate is an affine function of the corresponding mathematical coordinate, as in L29.

**Negative example.** For $(-3,-2)$ with the same parameters:

$$u=400+50(-3)=250,\qquad v=300-50(-2)=400.$$

The point is left of and below the centre. Two minus signs give a plus: moving down in the mathematical frame increases the row number.

### Inverse: undo the operations

Recover the point from $(500,250)$.

| Operation | Horizontal | Vertical |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Remove origin and respect vertical direction | $500-400=100$ | $300-250=50$ |
| Divide by scale | $100/50=2$ | $50/50=1$ |

We recover $(2,1)$. In general:

$$u-u_0=s_xx\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=\frac{u-u_0}{s_x},$$

$$v_0-v=s_yy\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y=\frac{v_0-v}{s_y}.$$

The inverse is exact for continuous coordinates and scales $s_x,s_y>0$. Different scales can distort shapes and angles; for example, a circle becomes an ellipse. To preserve shapes and angles (up to reflection), choose $s_x=s_y$.

Another origin: with $u=300+40x$ and $v=200-40y$, point $(-2,3)$ becomes $(220,80)$. Returning from $(380,240)$ gives $(2,-1)$.

### Transform a line

For two points, changes become $\Delta u=s_x\Delta x$ and $\Delta v=-s_y\Delta y$. The slope of a nonvertical line becomes:

$$\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta u}=-\frac{s_y}{s_x}m.$$

A vertical line remains vertical: the quotient is undefined. A horizontal line keeps zero slope. With equal scales, slope simply changes sign.

Example: for $y=2x+1$ with $u=300+40x$ and $v=200-40y$:

1. Substitute y: $v=200-40(2x+1)=160-80x$.
2. Isolate x: $x=(u-300)/40$.
3. Substitute: $v=160-80(u-300)/40=160-2u+600$.
4. Conclude: $v=760-2u$.

### Round only at the end

Continuous coordinates are not necessarily integer indices. In a small $9\times9$ screen with origin $(4,4)$ and two pixels per unit, compare two points.

| Mathematical point | Continuous position | Rounded index |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $(0.12,0.12)$ | $(4.24,3.76)$ | $(4,4)$ |
| $(0.18,0.18)$ | $(4.36,3.64)$ | $(4,4)$ |

Rounding to nearest here gives the same pixel for two distinct points. The inverse of $(4,4)$ gives $(0,0)$, not the original coordinates: information has been lost.

Rounding error is at most half a pixel per coordinate, or $1/(2s_x)$ and $1/(2s_y)$ in mathematical units. For halfway ties, the workshop chooses the greater integer. Check bounds **after** rounding; a continuous position inside may produce an out-of-bounds index, and a position slightly outside may produce a valid index. This depends on the chosen convention.

## 5. Practise — From point to line

**Easy.** With $(u_0,v_0)=(200,150)$ and $s_x=s_y=40$, transform $(-2,3)$, then return.

<details>
<summary>Check the answer</summary>

$u=200+40(-2)=120$ and $v=150-40\cdot3=30$. The inverse gives $x=(120-200)/40=-2$ and $y=(150-30)/40=3$. The displacements are 80 pixels left and 120 pixels up.

</details>

**Intermediate.** With $(u_0,v_0)=(400,300)$, $s_x=50$ and $s_y=25$, recover the mathematical point associated with $(500,350)$. What is the screen slope if mathematical slope is 2?

<details>
<summary>Check the answer</summary>

$x=(500-400)/50=2$ and $y=(300-350)/25=-2$. Screen slope is $-(25/50)\cdot2=-1$. The unequal scales also change the absolute value of the slope.

</details>

**Challenge.** In an image of $9\times9$ pixels, a continuous position is $(8.6,4)$. Round to nearest, check visibility, then explain why the inverse after rounding does not guarantee the initial position.

<details>
<summary>Show the worked solution</summary>

The position rounds to $(9,4)$. Index 9 is forbidden because columns range from 0 to 8. Multiple continuous positions can give the same integer index: rounding is not invertible.

</details>

## 6. Remember — Printable revision sheet

- Screen: u rightwards, v downwards; integer indices $0\le u<W$ and $0\le v<H$.
- Forward: $u=u_0+s_xx$, $v=v_0-s_yy$.
- Continuous inverse: $x=(u-u_0)/s_x$, $y=(v_0-v)/s_y$, with $s_x,s_y>0$.
- Screen slope of a nonvertical line: $-(s_y/s_x)m$.
- Method: change units, reverse vertical direction, shift origin, check the inverse, round last and check indices.
- Traps: forgetting the minus sign, confusing centre with corner, confusing continuous coordinates and indices, assuming rounding is invertible.
