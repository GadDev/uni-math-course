# Two points and intersections

Two distinct points determine a line; two lines pose another question: which points do they share? You will move from construction to solving a system. First revisit first-degree equations ([L25](../L25/)) and the role of slope ([L29](../L29/)).

## Objectives and prerequisites

- Calculate slope between two points.
- Find a line equation.
- Solve an intersection.
- Distinguish the three cases.
- Handle vertical lines.
- Analyse a parameter or context.

Prerequisites: L25, L29. Domains and restrictions stated below are part of every rule.

## 1. Understand — Two measurements, one relationship

<span id="pdf-40" class="source-topic-marker"></span>

### Two points: finding the Celsius–Fahrenheit conversion

It is 50 °F in Belval: do you need a coat? To answer, recover the temperature in Celsius. The PDF (pp. 40–43) gives two reference temperatures for water at normal atmospheric pressure:

| Situation | Celsius $C$ (input) | Fahrenheit $F$ (output) | Point $(C,F)$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Water freezes | 0 | 32 | $(0,32)$ |
| Water boils | 100 | 212 | $(100,212)$ |

The conversion is **affine**: each increase of 1 °C causes the same increase in °F, but 0 °C does not correspond to 0 °F. A line of the form $y=mx+p$ describes this rule. Here $x$ becomes $C$ and $y$ becomes $F$:

$$F=mC+p.$$

Our mission: determine the two numbers $m$ (change per degree) and $p$ (starting value when $C=0$). Then we can convert in both directions.

## 3. Calculate — One operation at a time

### Conversion: find m, then p

**Step A — Calculate slope.** From point A to point B:

$$\Delta C=100-0=100,\qquad \Delta F=212-32=180.$$

$$m=\frac{\Delta F}{\Delta C}=\frac{180}{100}=1{,}8.$$

Each additional degree Celsius therefore represents an increase of 1.8 °F.

**Step B — Calculate the intercept.** Point A belongs to the line: substitute 0 for C and 32 for F.

$$32=1{,}8\cdot0+p=0+p.$$

Thus $p=32$ and the full rule is $F=1{,}8C+32$.

**Step C — Check both observations.** At $C=0$, $F=32$. At $C=100$, $F=180+32=212$. Both points fit.

**Step D — Convert 20 °C.**

$$F=1{,}8\cdot20+32=36+32=68.$$

**Step E — Reverse the conversion: 50 °F.**

| Operation | Equality |
| --- | --- |
| Substitute 50 for F | $50=1{,}8C+32$ |
| Subtract 32 from both sides | $18=1{,}8C$ |
| Divide both sides by 1.8 | $10=C$ |

It is therefore 10 °C. Check: $1{,}8\cdot10+32=50$. In general $C=(F-32)/1{,}8$: undo the operations in reverse order.

### Two distinct points

For $A=(x_1,y_1)$ and $B=(x_2,y_2)$ with $x_1\ne x_2$, slope is $m=(y_2-y_1)/(x_2-x_1)$. Then $p=y_1-mx_1$. Reversing point order changes both quotient signs and preserves slope. If horizontal coordinates agree and points are distinct, the line is vertical. Repeating a single point does not determine a unique line.

### How to recover the equation and check boundary cases

State that both points lie on the line: $y_1=mx_1+p$ and $y_2=mx_2+p$. Subtraction gives $y_2-y_1=m(x_2-x_1)$. For $x_2\ne x_1$, this yields:

$$m=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1},\qquad p=y_1-mx_1.$$

The second formula simply isolates $p$ in the first equation. These are connected steps: first eliminate the constant, then recover it.

1. Compare the points. Identical points do not determine a unique line.
2. Distinct points with the same horizontal coordinate give vertical line $x=x_1$.
3. Otherwise calculate slope, subtracting in **the same order** in numerator and denominator.
4. Calculate the intercept, then substitute **both** points into the resulting equation.

### Guided example: construct, then check

For $A=(1,4)$ and $B=(3,10)$, the two changes are $10-4=6$ and $3-1=2$. Thus $m=6/2=3$. Substituting A in $y=mx+p$ gives $4=3\cdot1+p$, then $p=4-3=1$. The line is $y=3x+1$. Check: $3\cdot1+1=4$ and $3\cdot3+1=10$. An equivalent form is $y-y_1=m(x-x_1)$.

### Guided example: intersection

At the intersection of $y=2x+1$ and $y=-x+7$, the two y values agree.

$$2x+1=-x+7$$

Add x to both sides: $3x+1=7$. Subtract 1: $3x=6$. Divide by 3: $x=2$. Then $y=2\cdot2+1=5$. The other line also gives $-2+7=5$. The answer is the pair $(2,5)$.

For temperature, $F=C$ gives $C=1{,}8C+32$. Subtract $1{,}8C$: $-0{,}8C=32$. Divide by $-0{,}8$: $C=-40$. Then $F=-40$ and $1{,}8(-40)+32=-40$.

### How to solve an intersection

A common point satisfies both equations **at once**. For $y=m_1x+p_1$ and $y=m_2x+p_2$, equating the two expressions for the same vertical coordinate gives:

$$m_1x+p_1=m_2x+p_2\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(m_1-m_2)x=p_2-p_1.$$

Before dividing, examine $m_1-m_2$. If nonzero, calculate $x$, then $y$, and check both equations. If zero, compare intercepts: different intercepts give a contradiction; equal intercepts leave every point on the line. The formula for $x$ cannot be used in all three cases. For a vertical line, substitute its horizontal coordinate directly into the other equation.

In the activity, advance one step at a time: write both constraints, equate or substitute, collect without dividing too soon, then check the resulting point.

### The three cases and vertical lines

- $y=2x+1$ and $y=-x+4$: $3x=3$, giving $(1,3)$.
- $y=2x+1$ and $y=2x+5$: subtract $2x$ to get $1=5$, impossible.
- $y=2x+1$ and $2y=4x+2$: dividing the second by 2 gives the first. Every point on this line fits, not just $(0,0)$.
- $x=4$ and $y=-x+6$: substitute 4 for x to get $(4,2)$. Distinct verticals do not meet; identical verticals coincide.

In an application, also check the domain: a negative quantity or extrapolation may not have practical meaning.

## 5. Practise — From easy to transfer

**Easy.** Convert 30 °C to Fahrenheit.

<details>
<summary>Check my answer</summary>

$F=1{,}8\cdot30+32=54+32=86$. If you got 54, the starting value 32 is missing.

</details>

**Intermediate.** Find the line through $(0,2)$ and $(2,0)$, then its intersection with $y=x$.

<details>
<summary>Show the worked solution</summary>

$m=(0-2)/(2-0)=-1$; $p=2$ because $(0,2)$ lies on the line. Thus $y=-x+2$. Intersection requires $x=-x+2$, so $2x=2$, then $x=1$ and $y=1$. Check: $-1+2=1$. The two original points also give 2 and 0.

</details>

**Challenge.** For which real values of k do $y=2x+1$ and $y=kx+5$ have exactly one intersection?

<details>
<summary>Check the reasoning</summary>

Equating gives $(2-k)x=4$. If $k\ne2$, divide to obtain $x=4/(2-k)$, then $y=2x+1$. If $k=2$, we get $0=4$, impossible. Coincidence is excluded because the intercepts differ.

</details>

## 6. Remember — Printable revision sheet

- Distinct points with $x_A\ne x_B$: $m=(y_B-y_A)/(x_B-x_A)$, then $p=y_A-mx_A$. Check both points.
- Same horizontal coordinate, distinct points: $x=x_A$; identical points: no unique line.
- Two nonvertical lines: $(m_1-m_2)x=p_2-p_1$. Divide only if $m_1\ne m_2$; otherwise compare intercepts.
- Intersection: give both coordinates and verify both equations. An intersection outside the plot can exist.
- Conversion: $F=1{,}8C+32$ and $C=(F-32)/1{,}8$.
